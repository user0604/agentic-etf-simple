PJM Says Its Power Market Is Broken

PJM's capacity market was built for a world of predictable load growth and three-year construction timelines. Neither assumption holds anymore.

Nicholas Sciple

May 11, 2026, 9:40 AM

Comments
9.5

PJM Interconnection, the grid operator that keeps the lights on for 65 million people across 13 states and DC, published a 70-page white paper last week that every investor should pay attention to. PJM is openly admitting the market design that governed power generation investment for two decades is broken, and the fixes on the table could reshape who makes money in this sector for years to come.

PJM's capacity market was built for a world of predictable load growth and three-year construction timelines. Neither assumption holds anymore. Data center demand is growing at the fastest pace in nearly half a century, while building a new gas plant now takes four-plus years and costs over $2,200 per kilowatt, roughly double what it cost a decade ago. PJM's most recent auction cleared 6,500 megawatts short of the reliability target.

PJM is calling this a "credibility trap," and it works like this. When the grid is short on power, prices spike. Those high prices are supposed to attract investors to build new plants. The problem is that most retail customers buy electricity at rates that adjust quickly, so when wholesale prices jump, household bills jump too. Voters get angry, politicians step in with price caps and emergency measures, and investors watching from the sidelines conclude that the high prices they were counting on won't last. So they don't build. The shortage gets worse.

PJM's response is to lay out three possible paths. Path A would require utilities and retail electricity providers to lock in most of their power needs through long-term contracts years in advance, so customers aren't exposed to wild swings in the spot market. Path B would create a tiered reliability system, where new large customers like data centers get cut off first during shortages if they haven't brought their own generation to the table. Path C would shift how power plants earn their money, moving away from the current capacity payments and toward higher energy prices during scarcity, with long-term contracts protecting consumers from the volatility. PJM refused to recommend any one of these, which tells you how politically charged this is.

A few investment takeaways. First, building power plants on spec is essentially over. For years, developers would build a gas plant betting they could sell the power into the spot market at whatever price prevailed. PJM is saying in plain language that those days are done. Lenders now require generators to lock in buyers under long-term contracts before they'll finance the project. That favors regulated utilities and large power companies with established customer relationships, and it pushes out smaller independent developers who used to compete on speed and cost alone.

Second, gas turbine original equipment manufacturers, or OEMs, sit on a gold mine. Global orders are running 100 gigawatts a year against 60 gigawatts of production capacity. GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries own this bottleneck.

Third, the data center flexibility story is real. PJM is explicitly designing markets to pay hyperscalers for shifting AI training workloads when the grid is tight. That's a tailwind for behind-the-meter generation and storage providers.

Fourth, and this one matters most for my coverage, BWX Technologies (NYSE: BWXT) and the broader nuclear renaissance just got more validation. When PJM tells you it cannot build its way out of this deficit in the near term, and reliability requires long-term contracts, that's the case for any dispatchable, long-lived generation asset that can lock in an off-take agreement.

The capacity market that built PJM for two decades is being rewritten in real time. The winners will be companies that can sign 15-year contracts and deliver firm megawatts and those that own the bottlenecks to making that happen. Invest accordingly.
