Broadcom's AI Chips Soar, but Software Lags

The market wanted a blowout; Broadcom delivered a strong quarter.

Emily Flippen, CFA

9:55 AM

Comments
9.2

A few quarters ago, we told members to watch two things in Broadcom's (NASDAQ:AVGO) business: whether software demand could hold up alongside semiconductor growth, and the risk of hyperconcentrated customer growth. Unfortunately, both of those concerns showed up when Broadcom reported second quarter earnings last night - and while the market might be caught up in a miss in the headline numbers, we still think these two risks are the key points worth watching.

Let’s start with the quarter itself. Broadcom barely missed revenue expectations, something that likely wouldn’t be of note for another company if shares hadn’t rallied over 15% before earnings. It was clear the market was expecting a solid beat, and an otherwise strong quarter can’t make up for heightened expectations. However, 140%+ year-over-year AI semiconductor growth is nothing to turn our noses up at, even if the market is punishing shareholders this morning.

The good news is the AI chip revenue beat forecasts and guidance for the third quarter implies more than 200% year-over-year growth in semiconductor sales. Sales guidance for next quarter also came in better than feared, likely in part due to large XPU (custom chip) customers that now include Anthropic and OpenAI. Naturally, these two partnerships represent massive amounts of compute infrastructure, which can create lucrative opportunities in the future for Broadcom.

However, while these large relationships bring concentration risk, they also bring massive tailwinds for revenue. And this is where I find myself scratching my head: CEO Hock Tan has been consistently guiding for $100 billion in annual AI chip revenue by FY2027. Tan reiterated that goal on last night’s earnings call. However - with $8.4, $10.8, and $16 billion in chip revenue for the first, second, and guidance for third quarter 2026 - this implies that Broadcom is already on track to do north of $50 billion in total AI chip revenue this year alone. This means Broadcom is going to have to double its AI chip revenue over the next fiscal year. Partnerships like Anthropic and OpenAI can make that happen - especially with their publicly claimed commitments - but these deals haven't raised guidance. The fact that Broadcom has performed so well in AI chip sales but hasn’t adjusted its FY27 goal could also be part of the reason why we’re seeing the market act skeptically - these deals aren’t actually changing longer-term expectations for business performance, but rather derisking the expectations that were already built in for performance.

But as we flagged last quarter, on the software side of Broadcom’s business, we’ll need to see equally strong performance to prove it can turn custom chips into recurring revenue. Unfortunately, infrastructure software growth of 9% came in worse than the market feared, causing some hesitation about whether or not Broadcom can justify its valuation based on VMware alone. However, it’s worth noting that 9% growth is actually a significant improvement from the 1% growth that had us flagging yellow warning signs last year, and the VMware Cloud Foundation migration seems to be moving forward well (turning from perpetual licenses to subscriptions for recurring revenue). Over 90% of Broadcom’s largest 10,000 enterprise customers have accepted the migration. However, a quick caveat that explains why this metric is so important to watch: independent survey data has found that while license purchases for VMware are up, many customers may be consciously attempting to reduce their VMware footprint to save costs, so total VCF signed agreements may not show the warning signs of downsizing usage.

The idea behind the software + hardware combo of custom chips is that VMware should become more valuable as enterprises need to manage GPU clusters inside private cloud environments, an argument that seems to gain favor as AI increases cloud complexity by the day. However, the denominator of that equation (hardware) has been growing so fast that software's percentage of total revenue has compressed - falling to around 32% this quarter vs 44% a year ago. The market is reacting like Broadcom’s software business is deteriorating, but really it’s just getting massively eclipsed by rising chip sales. Broadcom still needs the software transition to work effectively to justify its valuation, but so far there are no true signs that it isn’t happening (in my opinion).

Moving forward, we’ll continue to watch that $100 billion FY27 commitment (and if Tan chooses to raise it in future quarters to explain massive deals), continued AI-based customer concentration, and of course software sales as a sign Broadcom can turn the near-term boom into a long-term growth story. For now, we think the market’s reaction to second-quarter results has much more to do with its near-term run up and a game of expectations than it does any true fundamental issues with Broadcom’s thesis.
