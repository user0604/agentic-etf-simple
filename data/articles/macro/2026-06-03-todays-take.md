THE QUESTION

What’s a common financial metric you don’t like to rely on — and what do you use instead?

1. Cash Is King

By Alicia Alfiere
TEAM RULE BREAKERS

Multiples are a shorthand for deeper valuation. Price-to-revenue multiples are helpful, especially if a company isn’t profitable or producing cash yet. But price-to-free cash flow is my favorite financial health indicator.

Companies generating cash have money left over after paying bills and can use that money to pay back debt, reinvest in the business, or return value to shareholders. I also believe companies producing cash tend to be sustainable over the long term. But multiples have limitations, and context matters. For example, a financially healthy company can show decreasing free cash flow as a result of self-funding growth instead of growing with debt — and that can impact the price-to-free cash flow metric too.

2. Operating Income Can Offer Another Perspective

By Jason Moser
TEAM RULE BREAKERS

I like to try to keep as open a mind as possible when it comes to investing. The traditional P/E ratio is something we see often quoted in financial media because it’s easy, and contrary to what some may believe, I do think it’s relevant.

However, I’ve also found that when building out a model, I like looking at price to operating income as another way to look at a company’s valuation. When you go down the income statement past operating income, all sorts of unique line items can come into play for any given company, and they’re not always constant. Operating income for me is just a cleaner way to view how the business is doing and understanding better what’s really driving profitability (or the lack thereof).

3. Watch Out for Those Adjustments

By Matt Frankel, CFP®
TEAM HIDDEN GEMS

There are many companies that rely on adjusted EBITDA to show how profitable they are, but I largely ignore this number when researching stocks. Charlie Munger once used some very colorful language when discussing adjusted EBITDA, and I don’t blame him. Adjusted EBITDA adds back stock-based compensation to the earnings calculation, which is a significant expense for many of the most popular fast-growing companies, and even on an EBITDA basis, it ignores important expenses (taxes and insurance) that are important components of how much money a company is actually making.

The recent SpaceX (NASDAQ: SPCX) IPO filing is a great example — the company posted a $4.9 billion loss in 2025 under generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) but reported a $6.6 billion profit in adjusted EBITDA. One figure tells a completely different story than the other.
