May Rankings: A New Dividend Low

Here are our 10 best ideas from the Dividend Investor scorecard.

Matthew Argersinger and Anthony Schiavone

May 28, 2026, 1:00 PM

Comments (4)
8.6

Dividend Investors,

This month, the stock market reached (or breached) a new record. And it's not one to necessarily celebrate if you're an income investor.

The dividend yield of the S&P 500 fell below 1%, the lowest level on record. The previous low was 1.1% in early 2000 -- at the peak of the dot-com bubble.

There are many ways to explain this new market milestone, and most aren't necessarily bad:

The stock market is at (or near) all-time highs; prices move in the opposite direction of yield.
Long-term Treasury yields recently hit their highest level in nearly two decades, making comparable dividend yields from riskier sources, such as publicly traded companies and real estate investment trusts (REITs), less appealing.
Many large-cap technology companies, which dominate the S&P 500's weighting, pay no or only very meager dividends.
By contrast, sectors like energy, real estate, and consumer staples, which have traditionally been higher sources of dividends, have shrunk as a share of the index.
Corporations generally earn higher returns on capital these days than in prior decades (partly a result of the U.S. transitioning from an industrial and manufacturing economy to a service economy), encouraging managers to reinvest earnings rather than pay them out to shareholders.
Public companies, especially over the last two decades, have placed greater emphasis on stock buybacks than on dividends (not a bad thing for shareholders).
The Buffett effect: Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway (NYSE: BRKB) has famously never paid dividends (at least since the late 1960's), and many other corporate executives have followed his lead.

These trends are probably not reversing or going away anytime soon. The market's dividend yield could head lower still.

That said, I find it hard to believe that a sub-1% dividend yield is some kind of sustainable new world order. I'm sure many investors may have believed that back in late 1999 or early 2000, only to see yields reverse during the dot-com crash and then surge higher in the lead-up to the global financial crisis.

History may not repeat. But it is certainly being made.

Read on for this month's top 10, where you will find plenty of dividend yields that are multiple times higher than the market's.

-- Matt Argersinger

1. TFS Financial

TFS Financial (NASDAQ: TFSL) is a $17.5 billion savings and loan institution offering a 7.2% dividend yield due to its unique mutual holding company structure. We believe that the combination of (1) an obscure security, (2) undervaluation, (3) improving fundamentals, (4) a resumption of share buybacks, (5) a second-step conversion "lottery ticket" and (6) a share price that's moving higher makes for a compelling investment.

To learn more about why we chose TFS Financial as May's recommendation in Dividend Investor, click here.

2. Domino's Pizza

Domino's Pizza (NASDAQ: DPZ) is off to a sluggish start on the Dividend Investor scorecard. Same-store sales in the U.S. grew just 0.9% in the first quarter versus the 2.5% growth expected by analysts polled by Visible Alpha, a S&P Global Market Intelligence company. This feels like a short-term headwind.

Competitors are offering more value deals that mimic Domino's recent promotions, creating a pricing war that will pressure near-term results. But with better franchisee economics, Domino's will likely be the long-term beneficiary of market share gains. In the meantime, Domino's trades for just 16 times forward earnings and should be able to return around 6% of its current market cap to shareholders through dividends and buybacks this year.

3. Owens Corning

Owens Corning (NYSE: OC) -- and anything related to residential real estate -- has sold off sharply in recent months as mortgage rates have moved higher. In the near term, expect some demand destruction for Owens Corning's discretionary and new-construction end markets. But this remains a cash-generative business.

While the company did not repurchase any shares during the working-capital-intensive first quarter, Owens Corning remains on track to return around $1 billion of capital to shareholders this year through dividends and buybacks. At Owens Corning's current valuation, we expect the sales proceeds from the recently completed divestiture of the glass reinforcements business to be plowed into share repurchases.

4. Masco

Masco (NYSE: MAS), our favorite manufacturer of paint and plumbing products, delivered a strong first-quarter earnings report. Sales grew 6%. Adjusted earnings per share grew 20%. And the company returned $267 million to shareholders through dividends and buybacks, representing about 2% of its average market cap during the quarter.

Additionally, the company announced a $300 million accelerated share repurchase program and expects to deploy "at least" $800 million (instead of the previous guidance of $600 million) toward buybacks and bolt-on acquisitions this year. At Masco's current valuation of 16 times forward earnings, let's hope capital is earmarked for the former rather than the latter.

5. ConocoPhillips

ConocoPhillips (NYSE: COP) delivered another impressive earnings report as it remains on track to deliver $7 billion of incremental free cash flow by 2029, an inflection that would double the free cash flow generated in 2025. Importantly, that scenario assumes a $70 West Texas Intermediate (WTI) oil price, which is lower than where longer-dated oil futures contracts trade today as the conflict in the Middle East continues.

On Conoco's first-quarter earnings call, CEO Ryan Lance said expected cash generation is up "materially" and that shareholders will directly benefit due to the company's commitment to return at least 45% of cash from operations by way of dividends and buybacks. Conoco remains one of the best growth stories in the oil patch.

6. The Hershey Company

Our thesis on The Hershey Company (NYSE: HSY) is finally starting to come to fruition. After a stratospheric rise in cocoa prices, the cost for the key ingredient used in chocolate is about 70% off its high.

That will eventually be a boon to Hershey. The company is unlikely to lower prices, so margins should expand nicely in the coming years. While Hershey's valuation of 23 times forward earnings today may look expensive, our guess is that the company trades closer to something like 17 times normalized earnings once it starts benefiting from cost deflation. If we're right, that's an attractive valuation for a company that will be one of the few growth stories in the consumer staples sector.

7. Simon Property Group

Simon Property Group (NYSE: SPG) had another Simon-esque quarter: Expectations were exceeded, guidance was raised, and the dividend was increased. Over the last 21 quarters, Simon has raised its dividend 15 times! With favorable supply-and-demand fundamentals for high-quality real estate, more income should be flowing shareholders' way in the coming quarters. Miraculously, even as its share price has nearly doubled over the last three years, Simon still trades at just 15 times the midpoint of management's forward real estate funds from operations guidance.

8. The Home Depot

It has been quite a while since The Home Depot (NYSE: HD) graced our rankings here in Dividend Investor. Not a lot has changed since. The leading home improvement retailer is still dealing with a sluggish housing market, tepid same-store sales growth, and a reluctance on the part of the customer to spend on big-ticket items.

But what has changed quite dramatically is Home Depot's stock price, which recently closed below $300 for the first time since 2023. A below-market price-to-earnings (P/E) multiple (off a depressed earnings base) and a near-3% dividend yield feel like a good entry point for a company that has done quite a bit of heavy lifting on the acquisition front in recent years to position itself for an eventual housing revival.

9. Everest Group

Sometimes, the best way to grow is to shrink. Having recently exited the commercial retail insurance business, Everest Group (NYSE: EG) is now focused on core reinsurance and wholesale specialty lines, where the firm has historically demonstrated far higher efficacy.

That was evidenced in Everest's first-quarter results, where gross written premium volume fell almost 19%, but Everest's combined ratio was a strong 91.2%, net operating income soared, and annualized return on equity nearly tripled to 16.8%. That improved profitability, and higher net investment income enabled Everest to repurchase over 1 million of its outstanding shares in the quarter for an average price of $330.

With shares currently trading around $350 against a book value per share of almost $384, expect management to continue to lower the share count. Keep shrinking, Everest!

10. Brookfield Asset Management

CEO Connor Teskey opened up Brookfield Asset Management's (NYSE: BAM) first-quarter call with a bang, saying, "2026 will not only be a record year for Brookfield, but one where we expect to exceed our long-term growth targets." Perhaps it's no surprise that Brookfield remains a fundraising machine, but there was one surprise during the report: buybacks.

Brookfield repurchased $375 million worth of shares in the first quarter, along with an additional $200 million in buybacks so far in the second quarter, as management believed shares were "meaningfully undervalued." That brings total buybacks to nearly $800 million over the last seven months. Along with a 4.2% dividend yield -- a payout that's expected to grow at a 15% annualized rate through the end of the decade -- Brookfield remains a top opportunity this month.
