Berkshire Finds Its Homebuilding Platform

Good for Berkshire shareholders. Good for Taylor Morrison shareholders. But man, I wish I'd found this one earlier!

Jim Gillies

Jun 1, 2026, 9:28 AM

Comments (1)
9.1

It's the first "major" acquisition of the Greg Abel era at Berkshire Hathaway (NYSE: BRKA)(NYSE: BRKB)!

Berkshire is acquiring homebuilder Taylor Morrison (NYSE: TMHC) for $72.50 in cash per share, a 24% premium to Friday's closing price. The price represents a market capitalization of of$6.8 billion, or $8.5 billion on an enterprise value basis (i.e., adding in net debt). Taylor Morrison operates in 12 states, mainly in the southwest and southeast (plus Texas), serving homebuyers across the ownership spectrum from entry-level to "resort lifestyle" (i.e., communities where you don't mind golf balls ricocheting off your siding).

Full disclosure, I'm coming before you today a little annoyed (at myself). Mainly because Taylor Morrison was right in my small cap wheelhouse – and yet I'd never heard of it until yesterday (whether that's ignorance on my part or just bad luck, you can decide). The gold standard in homebuilder investing is NVR (NYSE: NVR) due to their long-term outstanding track record of capital allocation that mainly focuses on using options to control land inventory, carefully husband cash deployment and employ minimal debt, and then use cash flows resulting from home sale completions to steadily reduce their share count via buybacks. Over the past 25 years, NVR shares have returned about 3,900% while the share count is down by more than 66%.

There have been multiple homebuilders trying to emulate NVR's success (looking at you, Dream Finders Homes (NYSE: DFH)) but none that really seemed to "get it". None, that is, until I took a brief look at Taylor Morrison this morning in the wake of the acquisition announcement. Revenues have been fairly flat for the last five years (4.2% annually), but that's no sin in an industry that's struggled as a whole (NVR has had 4.3% annual revenue growth over that same span). It's on the cash flow statement where Taylor Morrison shines – it's like looking at a mini-NVR! Lots of cash generation, where construction financing debt is used – it's almost immediately repaid, with excess cash flows going to share buybacks.

Over the past five years both NVR and Taylor Morrison have bought back around a quarter of their period-starting shares. Shareholder return-wise NVR has posted a 23% gain; Taylor Morrison has more than quintupled that performance, up 121%. Taylor Morrison looks like the exact type of company I'd like to bring into Hidden Gems Canada.

Now I can't; Berkshire is taking it. And look, Berkshire is my largest personal holding so, I'm happy in one sense – this looks like a well-run high-quality company, and I'm happy it's finding a "forever home" in the arms of Berkshire. Berkshire isn't exactly paying a high price either; that $8.5 billion enterprise value is roughly just 8x trailing EBITDA and 12.4x trailing FCF – in a difficult housing market, remember. That said, Taylor is going to be hard-pressed to really make a difference in Berkshire's consolidated financials. This is a trillion dollar company with nearly $400 billion in cash on the books after all; the price being paid for the entirety of Taylor Morrison's enterprise value is barely one third of the free cash flow (FCF) that Berkshire produced in 2025, a somewhat muted year for the mega-conglomerate.

Good for Berkshire shareholders. Good for Taylor Morrison shareholders. But man, I wish I'd found this one earlier!
