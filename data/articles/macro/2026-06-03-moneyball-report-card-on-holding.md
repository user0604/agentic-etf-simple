Moneyball Report Card: On Holding

The Motley Fool Team

Jun 3, 2026, 6:00 PM

Comments

What the Hidden Gems Primary database scores can tell us about On Holding.

In mid-May, On Holding (NYSE: ONON) posted record first-quarter net sales of 831.9 million Swiss francs -- more than 26% constant-currency growth -- and raised its full-year profit outlook, lifting its gross-margin target to at least 64.5% on the strength of booming Asia-Pacific and apparel demand. The same quarter, co-founders David Allemann and Caspar Coppetti officially stepped back in as co-CEOs, putting the people who built the brand back at the controls for its next chapter.

On pulled off something most performance brands only dream about: It convinced serious runners and fashion-conscious city dwellers to want the same shoe. The Swiss company's distinctive sole design became a genuine cultural signal, and the Moneyball database confirms the business underneath the hype is real. The question it raises isn't whether On can sell shoes; it's who actually controls the company you'd be buying into.

Growth is the obvious strength, and it's earned. A Growth score of 85 and Financial 5Y of 79 capture a remarkable five-year arc: Revenue scaled from under $800 million to nearly $3 billion, through early-stage losses into consistent profitability by 2023. The most recent year added 24.2% revenue growth across both wholesale and direct-to-consumer channels. Crucially, this growth comes with premium economics -- gross margins north of 62% -- and a conservative balance sheet with low leverage and healthy liquidity. This brand is compounding at scale while funding its own expansion.

The product story explains why. A Product 1Y score of 69 reflects On's real competitive moat: the rare merger of high-performance technical footwear with fashion-forward design, capturing two customer bases at once. Global expansion, a maturing omnichannel retail presence, an expanding apparel line, and high-profile luxury-tier collaborations have all kept consumer interest high. The brand equity here is the asset, and On has managed it deftly.

Two honest caveats temper the picture. The first is the nature of the moat itself. It rests heavily on brand strength and design, which demand constant vigilance against imitation and shifting tastes. The database flags a notable absence of reported R&D spending and an AI score of just 8, a reminder that On's collaborations are about brand visibility, not deep technological differentiation. There's also early concern that retail discounting could erode the premium positioning that makes the whole model work. For a brand company, brand risk is the business risk.

The sharper flag is ownership. The Owner score of 54 is the lowest in this batch for a reason: On's dual-class share structure concentrates voting power within the extended founder team, leaving outside shareholders with limited influence over key decisions. The database is blunt that economic interest and voting power are misaligned -- public investors put up capital without a proportionate say. Founder control can align management with long-term value, and the co-founders' return to the executive helm signals commitment to the brand's core strategy. But concentrated control also carries entrenchment risk, and recent leadership transitions add a layer of organizational uncertainty. Governance scores a respectable 76, helped by progress remediating past internal-control weaknesses, but the voting structure is the durable issue.

So the central question for On is one of trust. The brand is genuinely strong, the financials are premium, and the growth runway across global markets and apparel is real. At a 1.1x 200-week average multiple, the valuation isn't an obstacle. What investors are really deciding is whether they're comfortable owning the economics of a fast-growing brand while the founders keep the steering wheel. For believers in the brand and the team, that's a feature. For everyone else, it's the reason to read the fine print twice.
