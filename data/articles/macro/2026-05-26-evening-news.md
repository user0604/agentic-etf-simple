1. Zscaler Sweats the Competition

The cybersecurity gold rush is getting crowded, and Stock Advisor holding Zscaler (NASDAQ: ZS) is feeling it. The cloud security firm guided fourth-quarter revenue to between $875 million and $878 million, just shy of analysts' $878.6 million estimate, sending shares down 15% in extended trading.

Bigger fish are circling: Zscaler competes in the Secure Access Service Edge (SASE) market -- one of cybersecurity's fastest-growing segments, since SASE bundles networking and security into a single cloud platform. The problem? Heavyweights like Palo Alto Networks (NASDAQ: PANW) are muscling in with their own platform offerings as enterprises tighten cloud security budgets.
Costs are climbing fast: Third-quarter revenue of $850.5 million beat estimates, but operating expenses jumped roughly 25% to $687.5 million from $547.5 million a year ago. Adjusted Q4 profit guidance of $1.08 to $1.09 per share did top the $1.03 estimate, so it's not all bad news.
2. Altman Walks Back the "Job Apocalypse"

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman now thinks artificial intelligence (AI) won't trigger the mass white-collar layoffs he once warned about -- and he says he's "delighted to be wrong." Speaking at a Commonwealth Bank of Australia (ASX: CBA) conference on Tuesday, Altman admitted entry-level office jobs haven't been eliminated at anywhere near the pace he expected.

The "human part" stuck around: Altman tried having AI answer his Slack and email messages with a "this is Sam's AI" tag, then went back to doing it himself. His takeaway: Human interaction is harder to outsource than he thought, and "the jobs picture is likely to be very different than we thought."
Tell that to corporate HR: HSBC, Amazon (NASDAQ: AMZN), and Standard Chartered have all announced AI-driven job replacements. Meanwhile, OpenAI is reportedly preparing to confidentially file for a U.S. initial public offering (IPO) in the coming weeks, with a potential $1 trillion valuation and at least $60 billion raise on the table.
3. Uber and Lyft, Meet the Union

Roughly 70,000 Massachusetts ride-share drivers for Uber (NYSE: UBER) and Lyft (NASDAQ: LYFT) just got the first officially recognized gig worker union in the country. The App Drivers Union received certification from the state's Department of Labor Relations on Friday, capping a years-long fight over whether app drivers count as contractors or employees. Uber is currently recommended in Rule Breakers.

Costs about to climb: Drivers can now collectively bargain over pay and benefits, which threatens to squeeze margins for Uber and Lyft in one of their bigger U.S. markets. The companies already pay a $32.50 hourly minimum in Massachusetts after a 2024 settlement, and a union contract could push wages and benefits higher from there.
The dominoes are lining up: California passed similar legislation in October, Illinois has a bill pending, and labor leaders are pointing to Massachusetts as the template. Uber and Lyft didn't fight the ballot measure, and Lyft says it's committed to "engaging in good faith."
