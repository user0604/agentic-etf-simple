May Review: Software and Solar Soared

The technology sector, especially software stocks, returned 20% in May. That lifted indexes to new highs, despite eight of 11 sectors ending the month in the red.

Commentary: Five Takeaways From May
1. Major Indexes at All-Time Highs

The S&P 500 returned 5.3% in May, extending its streak of weekly gains to nine. According to The Wall Street Journal, the 16.1% return of the S&P 500 over the course of April and May is the fifth-best two-month return for the index since 1950.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average returned 2.9%, while the Russell 2000 index of small-cap stocks returned 4.4%.

But the big winner in May was the tech-dominated Nasdaq, which returned 8.3%. The total return for both April and May was 25%, its best two-month return since the early 2000s.

Each index ended the month at an all-time high.

2. Tech Makes Up for Sagging Sectors

Technology stocks gained an extraordinary 19.8% in May, their best month since 2001. The sector got a big boost from the rebound in software stocks. The iShares Expanded Tech-Software Sector ETF (NYSEMKT: IGV) returned 21.2% last month.

Those remarkable gains pulled up the rest of the market. Despite the major U.S. stock indexes rising last month, eight of 11 sectors ended May in the red.

The sector seeing the biggest decline was energy, which dropped 5.6%. The price of oil fell almost 20% in May, its largest monthly decline since 2020. Prices declined due to optimism that the United States and Iran would reach an agreement to reopen the Strait of Hormuz.

However, industry executives are warning that oil prices may go higher. CNBC reported that at a recent conference, Exxon (NYSE: XOM) Senior Vice President Neil Chapman said, "We're approaching unheard of inventory levels. I mean really, really low levels. You can debate whether that's going to hit, those really low levels, in two weeks or three weeks. Once you get to that point, then you'll see price shoot up."

However, not all energy-related stocks powered down in May. The Invesco Solar ETF (NYSEMKT: TAN) soared 26.6%. It was the best month for solar stocks since 2013.

3. Interest Rates Fluctuate, Inflation on the Rise

Yields on Treasuries rose through the first half of the month. On May 19, the yield on the 10-year Treasury reached 4.6% and the 30-year Treasury hit 5.2%. That was the highest yield for the latter in 20 years.

Yields drifted down from those levels by the end of the month, but the concerns that led to higher rates remain. One of the biggest is inflation. The personal consumption expenditures price index (PCE) increased 0.4% in April, or 3.8% year over year. Excluding food and energy, core prices rose 0.2% for the month and 3.3% for the year. Those 12-month figures are the highest since 2023.

4. Bonds Flat-ish for the Year

Despite the rate gyrations, the bond market eked out a 0.3% return in May, bringing its year-to-date return to 0.5%.

As I recently wrote in "Bonds Are Back to Singing the Blues," favor high-yielding cash or cash equivalents for money you need in the next one to two years, and CDs, individual bonds, or target-date bond ETFs for money you need in the next few to several years. Such a strategy will provide more predictability than the standard bond fund.

Another consideration for the safer side of your portfolio is Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS), especially if you believe inflation will keep rising. The yields on TIPS have been trending upward in 2026, and near or at levels not seen since the early 2010s.

Keep in mind that the yield on TIPS is what you'll earn on top of inflation if you hold them to maturity. They're quirky investments, so to learn more about them (as well as their equally quirky cousins, I bonds), read "TIPS, I Bonds: Safe, Inflation-Beating Investments."

5. Rebalance After May but Don't Go Away

As long-term investors, we at The Motley Fool don't put too much stock (pun intended) in short-term trading strategies or old Wall Street aphorisms. One such adage that always shows up this time of year is "sell in May and go away," which posits that an investor should stay out of the market from May to Halloween.

Indeed, the May to October stretch has historically been weaker. According to Dow Jones Market Data, since 1928 the S&P 500 has averaged 2.4% during the six-month period ending in October, compared with an average 5.2% in the six-month period ending in April.

But that trend has mostly reversed over the past decade, with the May-to-October period slightly outperforming since 2016. Plus, as previously discussed, this past May was pretty solid.

However...

As the market has reached new highs, market valuations have also neared levels not seen since the peak of the dot-com bubble. Norbert Keimling of Taunus Trust posted on LinkedIn the results of a recent analysis he did on the relationship between the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio (CAPE), popularized by Yale economist Robert Shiller, and subsequent stock market returns. The results: "[A]t today's Shiller CAPE of 39 for the U.S. market, I find no historical period since 1881(!) that was followed by attractive long-term real [inflation-adjusted] returns."

If you're an experienced investor with a high risk tolerance and a long timeline until you need to turn your stocks into cash, then this may be just noise. After all, valuations aren't destiny; they can be justified by accelerated earnings growth and profit margins near all-time highs.

But for those near or in retirement, this might be a good time to reevaluate how much of your portfolio should be protected in safer cash and bonds -- while still keeping a significant portion of your portfolio invested in stocks.
