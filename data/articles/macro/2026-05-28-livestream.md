Core Inflation Holds at 3.3%, No Cuts Soon

10:00 am

The Fed’s preferred inflation gauge, the PCE price index, rose 0.4% in April and 3.8% over the past year. Economists had expected a 0.5% monthly gain and a 3.8% annual rate, so the monthly read came in softer than feared.
Core PCE, which strips out food and energy, climbed 0.2% on the month and 3.3% year-over-year. The annual figure matched forecasts, while the monthly pace ran below the 0.3% economists penciled in.
The monthly numbers came in cooler than expected on both headline and core, which offers some relief after March’s hot 0.7% jump. But the annual readings haven’t budged. At 3.8% headline and 3.3% core, inflation sits nearly two percentage points above the Fed’s 2% target, and the path lower has stalled out for months.
The composition is what should worry investors most. Strip out food, energy, and housing, and core services rose just 0.2%, suggesting underlying demand-driven inflation is contained. The pressure is coming from energy spillovers tied to the Iran war and tariff effects feeding through to goods prices. Housing, which the Fed has long expected to cool, is heating up instead. That mix is harder for monetary policy to fix.

8:45 am — P -14.94% in pre-market trading

★ P is recommended in Stock Advisor (Team RB)

By Morning Show host Emily Flippen, CFA
TEAM RULE BREAKERS

Everpure (NYSE: P) — formerly Pure Storage — just reported a monster first quarter…. and the stock still sold off. Revenue grew 35% year-over-year to over $1 billion, operating profit nearly doubled, and management sizably raised full-year guidance. So what’s the problem? The problem is that a considerable chunk of this quarter’s strength was fueled by the most extreme supply chain crisis the semiconductor industry has seen in decades, and investors are trying to figure out how much of Everpure’s growth is real versus a byproduct of panic buying and price inflation.

Management was refreshingly transparent about this on the earnings call. CFO Tarek Robbiati noted that roughly a third of the quarter’s year-over-year revenue growth came from a combination of price increases and customer pull-ins, as buyers rushed to lock in products ahead of higher costs. That still leaves two-thirds attributable to volume and competitive wins, which is nothing to scoff at. CEO Charlie Giancarlo went even further, calling the current environment unlike anything he’s seen in over 40 years in tech, with spot prices for some components doubling "every 18 days" and long-term supply contracts effectively worthless at prior pricing levels.

What may be confounding investors today is the fact that Everpure is making a deliberate choice to eat some of this margin pain rather than pass the full cost along to customers. Giancarlo published a letter to customers in April essentially promising that Everpure would raise prices later and by less than the competition. Product gross margins came in at 65.5%, right at the low end of the 65% to 70% target range, and management was clear they intend to stay near the bottom while this crisis persists. While this may sound counterintuitive (and the market is certainly punishing the stock for that choice) the decision may make more sense over the long-term than Everpure is currently getting credit for. Maintaining the perspective of affordability during this crisis may help management not only protect its brand, but also win market share and build loyalty that may outlast the current supply shortage. So far it seems like that’s a strong choice: contract wins have risen, new customer logos grew 20% year-over-year, and Everpure is now in 64% of Fortune 500 companies.
