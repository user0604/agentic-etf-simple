1. Love It All … Except the Price Tag

By Matt Frankel, CFP®
TEAM HIDDEN GEMS

I’m not saying that Mastercard (NYSE: MA) isn’t a smart investment right now. But this is a company that rarely goes on sale (for good reason), so if I could scoop up shares at a 30% discount, I’d jump at the chance. To be sure, Mastercard is nearly 20% below its 52-week high, but at 29 times earnings, it would take a significant downward move from here to look genuinely attractive.

Mastercard is a business where, other than its valuation, I love pretty much everything about it. It has a high-margin and capital-light business model with no credit risk. It is part of an effective duopoly. It has a massive global market opportunity. And it has a management team that is being proactive about future financial technologies.

2. Berkshire at Book Value

By Anthony Schiavone

“Cash combined with courage in a time of crisis is priceless.” — Warren Buffett

I’ve always wanted to own Berkshire Hathaway (NYSE: BRKB) simply because I could say the greatest investor of all time managed my money. I never pulled the trigger.

But Berkshire, now led by Greg Abel, is back on my radar. Berkshire trades at a reasonable valuation today — so I’m getting a little greedy here — but I would be a likely buyer if the stock sold off 30%. (Yes, it would likely take a crisis of some sort for that to happen.)

Berkshire would then be trading near book value, a level last seen during the pandemic. With a portfolio of diverse and durable operating businesses and massive cash reserves, Berkshire will have the opportunity to invest countercyclicly and be a liquidity provider to a market that would likely be starved for capital. That’s a fat pitch.

3. Unlocking Value in the Internet of Things

By Jason Moser
TEAM RULE BREAKERS

I recommended Samsara (NYSE: IOT) back in 2023, and the stock has done well overall, though it’s been a volatile ride. Samsara is the pioneer of the Connected Operations Platform, which allows businesses that depend on physical operations to harness Internet of Things data to develop actionable insights and improve their operations.

Thanks to its large suite of services and fast ROI for customers, the company continues to grow at impressive rates, and I also appreciate that the co-founders own more than 32% of the shares outstanding; a meaningful stake to be sure. But as it’s still working toward profitability and meaningful cash flow, valuation remains one of the bigger risks today. A 30% haircut (depending on the reasons) would certainly pique my interest.

4. The Stock I’d Love to Buy: ARM

By Tim Beyers
TEAM RULE BREAKERS

I’ve been watching ARM Holdings (NASDAQ: ARM) for a while now. It’s precisely the sort of stock I’d love to own at a better price.

ARM intrigues me for two reasons. First, the company’s mobile chip designs control a dominant portion of the global smartphone market. Second, ARM’s well-regarded server compute designs operate at low power.

At a time of unprecedented data center build-out — and a limited supply of power to serve all that forthcoming capacity — ARM’s efficient chipsets strike me as essential. And yet the stock trades for near 100 times earnings and over 100 times cash flow. Give me a 30% discount from those heady multiples, and I’ll get interested quickly.

If the stock does well over the long run, the 30% difference in per share price will become (relatively) insignificant--on a per share basis.   That only matters if you plan to buy a fixed number of shares.  But at 30% off, you can buy almost 43% more shares for a given initial investment.

Consider Amazon at $3.83 at the end of 1999; 30% off that is $2.68.  If you buy 10 shares on 1/1/2000, your cost is $38.30 at the actual price and $26.80 at the discounted price.

The price of Amazon today is (say) $260.  If you hold your ten shares until today, you can sell them for $2,600.  That's a profit of $2561.70 at the full price and $2573.20 at the discounted price, a difference of $11.15, or less than 0.5%.  Who cares?

Now consider that you initially spend $38.30 at the start of 2000 for however many shares that will get you.  You can buy 10 shares at the full price ($38.30) and 14.28 shares at the discounted price.  Ten shares today are worth $2600 but 14.28 shares today are worth $3712.80, a difference of almost 43%.  Who wouldn't care?

In other words, differences in per share prices are not so important in and of themselves (as you correctly note) but for the effect they have on how much stock you can purchase.  The significance of that difference doesn’t diminish over time.
