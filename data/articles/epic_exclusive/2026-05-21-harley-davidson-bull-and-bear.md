Harley-Davidson Bull and Bear

The Motley Fool Team

May 21, 2026, 7:09 AM

Comments

Harley-Davidson, Inc. (NYSE:HOG) is an iconic American motorcycle manufacturer known for heavyweight cruising and touring bikes, and a wide range of Parts & Accessories (P&A). The company’s new “Back to the Bricks” strategic plan marks a decisive shift: Harley-Davidson is refocusing on brand authenticity, broader portfolio appeal (with new models like the Sportster returning in 2027 and Sprint arriving in 2026), cost discipline, and dealer profitability to reignite growth and restore long-term shareholder value.

Potential Bull Cases
1. Strategic Brand Renewal Targets Younger and Broader Customer Bases

Expanded model range and targeted marketing are set to revitalize demand among new and younger riders. Harley-Davidson’s launch of the “Back to the Bricks” plan pivots the company away from an exclusive reliance on touring and electric models, emphasizing accessibility and customization. The upcoming Sprint (2026) and revived Sportster (2027) aim to regain relevance with entry-level and younger customers—a segment historically pivotal for the brand’s growth. By integrating rider training and launching “blank canvas” models primed for customization, the company addresses concerns over aging demographics, aiming to democratize Harley-Davidson’s appeal globally. Success here can drive incremental unit sales and cross-selling in P&A, making youth engagement a potential volume and margin lever for years ahead.

2. Aggressive Cost Reduction and Operational Reset Improve Margin Prospects

Material cost savings and efficiency initiatives are poised to drive margin expansion over the next two years. Management has committed to at least $150 million in annual run-rate cost savings by 2027, underway with headcount reductions and streamlining of non-headcount costs. These moves have begun to realign both company and dealer inventories, which fell 22% year over year globally in Q1 2026. While gross margin contracted temporarily to 25.3% from 29.1% (Q1 2026 vs. Q1 2025; GAAP), Harley-Davidson targets restoring gross margins close to 30% as cost actions build and tariff pressures ease. Sustained execution of this cost-out agenda can unlock higher profitability, support reinvestment, and fortify the company against external shocks.

3. Dealer Network Profitability Anchors a More Resilient Business Model

Structured improvements to dealer economics will reinforce sales channels and protect franchise health. Dealer profitability now stands as a central—and “non-negotiable”—metric in Harley-Davidson’s transformation. Shifts like the fixed holdback program have already improved dealers’ cash flow predictability and staffing stability. The company is also focusing on fast-moving inventory, aiming for fresher models and healthier channel economics. This approach minimizes channel risk, ensures consistent customer experience, and boosts Harley-Davidson’s competitive advantages versus peers reliant on weaker or more fractured distribution networks.

4. Capital-Light Financial Services Model Unlocks Cash Flow and Lowers Risk

The transition of Harley-Davidson Financial Services (HDFS) to a forward-flow model releases capital for growth and returns. In 2025, Harley-Davidson sold much of its loan book to KKR and PIMCO, converting its financial services arm from a direct lender to a servicer and fee earner. In Q1 2026, HDFS revenue fell 54% due to this shift, but the move reduces balance sheet risk and enabled the company to redeploy capital previously tied up, with the deal freeing up about $1.25 billion. These freed resources support buybacks, dividends, product reinvestment, or opportunistic M&A—flexibility that can be decisive in a cyclical industry. Long-term targets call for $125–150 million in segment operating income by 2029, offering both efficiency and optionality as economic and regulatory conditions evolve.

5. Aftermarket and Lifestyle Expansion Enhance Growth and Resilience

Parts & Accessories (P&A) and brand extensions offer high-margin, sustainable growth drivers beyond core motorcycles. Harley-Davidson is targeting 20–30% P&A sales growth in the coming years, rejuvenating SKUs and investing in customization options that support dealer health and brand stickiness. Initiatives such as reintroducing 30% of recently eliminated P&A products and boosting aftermarket sales are designed to offset motorcycle demand cyclicality. Collaborations like the Dickies x Harley-Davidson apparel collection further broaden revenue streams and reinforce lifestyle status, cementing the brand’s cultural relevance. This diversification not only smooths earnings volatility, but also amplifies customer engagement and long-term franchise value.

Potential Bear Cases
1. Tariff and Recall Headwinds Are Eroding Earnings Power

Elevated tariff expenses and costly recalls have driven a sharp drop in profitability, with uncertain near-term relief. In Q1 2026, Harley-Davidson’s net income plunged 81% year over year (GAAP: $25 million vs. $133 million), with $45 million attributed to increased tariffs and significant costs from multiple major recalls, including recalls covering 88,039 and nearly 17,000 motorcycles. While recent regulatory exemptions help, 2026 full-year tariff costs are still forecast at a steep $75–90 million. These challenges weigh heavily on gross and operating margins, put strain on dealer economics, and risk eroding hard-won brand loyalty—especially if recalls keep making headlines or macro tensions hold tariffs high. Until these headwinds are demonstrably reduced, near-term financial recovery faces significant obstacles.

2. Restructured Financial Services Segment Reduces Short-Term Profitability and Predictable Cash Flow

The forward-flow, capital-light model at HDFS shrinks Harley-Davidson’s profit base while exposing the company to reliance on external partners. While transitioning most loan originations to KKR/PIMCO has lowered risk and freed up capital, Harley-Davidson’s financial services revenue plummeted in Q1 2026 (down 54% year over year) and contributed to the company missing consensus EPS estimates ($0.22 actual vs. $0.34 expected, GAAP). The reduction of interest income and less predictable servicing flows can limit the company’s ability to offset soft spots in core manufacturing earnings, especially during downturns. The shift, though strategically sensible long-term, poses short- and intermediate-term risk to cash flow consistency and segment profitability at a time of broader margin compression.

3. Macro and Competitive Pressures Continue to Constrain Growth Prospects

Consumer headwinds, analyst skepticism, and adverse global conditions dampen the stock’s near-term upside. Despite a recent rally in the company’s share price (+38%), analyst sentiment remains cautious given weak earnings quality, ongoing inflation, high interest rates, and geopolitical uncertainty affecting discretionary purchases—particularly for high-priced motorcycles. International sales declined 4% in Q1 2026 even as North America delivered double-digit retail gains, and macroeconomic pressures show no sign of abating. These dynamics, combined with rising short interest and fierce competition in both legacy and emerging electric motorcycle spaces, risk capping Harley-Davidson’s medium-term unit growth and profitability until broader conditions markedly improve or execution significantly outpaces expectations.
