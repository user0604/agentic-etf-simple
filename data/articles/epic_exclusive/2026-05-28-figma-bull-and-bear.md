Figma Bull and Bear

The Motley Fool Team

May 28, 2026, 7:09 AM

Comments

Figma, Inc. (NYSE:FIG) is a cloud-native, collaborative design and product development platform serving designers, engineers, and broader product teams around the world. The company’s strategy hinges on “land and expand”—driving organic adoption inside organizations and rapidly layering on enterprise agreements, all while pioneering AI-driven design and developer handoff. Figma’s rapid product innovation and emerging AI monetization are central to its bid for enduring leadership in the collaborative software stack.

Potential Bull Cases
1. Viral Enterprise Adoption Combined With Record Upsell Drives Durable Growth

Figma’s business model is demonstrating powerful expansion within existing customers and rapid acquisition of new ones. Q1 2026 showed a 54% year-over-year increase in paid customers (to approximately 690,000), with revenue growing 46% year over year to $333 million. Importantly, “land and expand” is translating into material upsell, as seen by the 37% year-over-year growth in customer cohorts spending more than $10,000 annual recurring revenue (ARR), and a 48% increase in those above $100,000. The net dollar retention (NDR) rate hit 139%, its highest point in over two years, demonstrating strong account expansion and adoption of premium features, particularly in the enterprise. This broad-based momentum suggests a resilient, compounding revenue base even as the company globalizes further, with international revenue up 48% year over year. Investors should note that in cloud software, NDR at this level is exceptional and historically correlates with sustainable hyper-growth and category leadership.

2. Successful AI Monetization Unlocks a New, High-Margin Revenue Stream

The rapid and sticky uptake of AI features is expanding Figma’s revenue model beyond seats and into usage-based monetization. After introducing AI credit limits in March 2026, over 75% of enterprise users who exceeded their initial limits chose to pay for additional credits, and over 95% stayed active post-enforcement. Teams purchasing AI add-ons spend more than three times as much annually, reflecting effective upsell and strong willingness to pay for productivity tools. The AI surface area keeps expanding through new features—such as the AI Assistant—and is expected to scale revenue further in upcoming quarters, with management explicitly citing AI monetization as a key reason for raising full-year guidance by $55 million to $1.42–1.43 billion. Because usage-based models can scale rapidly without commensurate sales hiring or onboarding costs, Figma stands to gain operating leverage as adoption widens across its installed base.

3. Category-Defining Product Innovation Shields Against Competitive Disruption

Relentless product development positions Figma at the forefront of collaborative design, even as new AI-native entrants emerge. In Q1 2026, the company unveiled major new capabilities, including the launch of Figma Make with contextualized prompt control, a model-agnostic control platform now with file read/write agents, expanded Weave video editing, and the debut of a truly native AI assistant. These releases are credited with a 5x quarter-over-quarter increase in weekly actives for new AI features and strong stickiness among customers (60% of large customers use Make weekly). This innovative pace—not just marketing hype—is critical as the design tooling space sees fast followers and disruptive new paradigms. Investors gain confidence from Figma’s ability to launch, monetize, and integrate new workflows faster than AI-native upstarts or entrenched giants can respond.

4. Robust Profitability and Cash Flow Enable Aggressive, Disciplined Investment

Figma is delivering growth alongside impressive financial discipline, providing room for strategic reinvestment without sacrificing cash health. Q1 2026 saw a non-GAAP operating margin of 16% and a 27% free cash flow margin, with $1.6 billion in cash and equivalents on the balance sheet. Even after a $56 million one-time bonus expense, the business generated positive non-GAAP earnings and topped consensus estimates by $0.27 per share. Gross margin remains high at 82%, and management has articulated clear levers (first-party AI models, query routing) to absorb increased AI-related costs. This margin profile gives Figma firepower to fund R&D, expand internationally, and defend its ecosystem against well-capitalized rivals for years to come.

5. Deep Integration and Network Effects Position Figma as “The System of Record” for Software Creation

Expanding workflows, integrations, and viral organizational adoption make Figma increasingly irreplaceable across product teams globally. Figma’s platform is now a core hub for not only designers but engineers, product managers, and operational staff at leading companies such as Google, Rocket Mortgage, and Lufthansa. Deeply integrated with enterprise software stacks, Figma’s multiplayer canvas, real-time commenting, and developer handoff functionality drive cross-team engagement and create high switching costs. As Figma’s tools become further entwined with adjacent platforms and business processes, its perceived value grows—entrenching it as the “system of record” where modern digital products are imagined, prototyped, and delivered. This confers powerful network effects and enables durable pricing and customer lock-in over time.

Potential Bear Cases
1. Intensifying AI-Native Competition Threatens Figma’s Long-Term Moat

Emerging AI-powered design platforms and virtual coworkers pose credible risks to Figma’s dominance. The launch of Anthropic’s Claude Design and well-funded startups like Viktor signal a new era of competition, where novel design paradigms and prompt-to-interface automation could erode the value of Figma’s traditional collaborative workflow. While Figma’s Q1 performance alleviated immediate obsolescence fears, these challengers are innovating quickly, especially among customers who prize automation or low-code/no-code solutions. If incumbents like Adobe and new entrants continue to blur the boundaries between design, code, and content, Figma could see its product become less differentiated, especially if network effects weaken at the periphery. Over time, such disruption could compress margins, stall upsell, and force Figma into more aggressive pricing wars.

2. Transition to Usage-Based AI Monetization Carries Structural and Cost Risks

Shifting from a purely seat-based to a hybrid consumption model introduces uncertainty around revenue predictability and profitability. Early AI credit monetization trends are positive, but the company is still in the early innings of customer education and adoption. There is a real risk that usage-based billing, especially if not well understood, could frustrate buyers used to predictable seat pricing, potentially leading to budget overruns, churn, or slower expansion. Managing AI inference costs is another challenge; if Figma cannot optimize cloud AI expenses quickly enough, or if model provider pricing escalates unpredictably, gross margins could see material downside. Furthermore, exposure to third-party model providers introduces regulatory and operational risks—ongoing legal disputes involving key AI vendors could directly impact Figma’s feature stability and cost structure.

3. Execution and Key-Person Risk Remain Material As Figma Scales Rapidly

Figma’s growth and innovation story are closely tied to a visionary, founder-driven leadership ethos that entails both upside and vulnerability. CEO Dylan Field’s personal involvement in product direction and market response has served the company well, but also creates key-person concentration risk. As Figma juggles international expansion, product complexity, and a transition in monetization models, any disruption in leadership or operational missteps could slow decision-making and erode morale, especially if faster, flatter teams become difficult to maintain at scale. Maintaining user “love” while aggressively monetizing AI, balancing hiring discipline with product velocity, and managing dilution all become more challenging as the company matures. The bull narrative assumes flawless execution; any cracks could reduce growth and dampen investor enthusiasm.
