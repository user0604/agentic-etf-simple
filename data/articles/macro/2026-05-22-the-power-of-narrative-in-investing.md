The Power of Narrative in Investing

Jim Mueller, CFA

May 22, 2026, 6:25 AM

Comments
9.3

Between 1928 and 2024, when the S&P 500 went up for the day, it averaged a gain of 0.75% per day. When it went down, it averaged a drop of 0.78% per day. For all the days, it averaged a gain of about 0.03% to 0.04%. Three to four basis points.

Over the same time period, if you (and probably your family going back a couple of generations) had invested in the S&P 500 and just held on, the total gain would be nearly 40,000%.

Which one is more compelling?

The second one, right? Why?

Before we answer that, let me throw out another example.

In a massive A/B test for charitable giving, hundreds of thousands of envelopes were sent out to potential donors, all with the same pitch. One-quarter of them were hand-delivered by a volunteer. One-quarter included a form to receive a 25% tax rebate. One-quarter were mailed in higher quality envelopes. And, you guessed it, one-quarter were in a different format.

Which one got the highest response rate?

You'd think it'd be the tax rebate because we know that incentives are important. But it was the one in the higher quality envelope.

Why?

Humans are pretty superficial. A better pitch, even if that's just the quality of the envelope, gets us every time.

That 40,000% return is the more compelling version, even though that's what you get if you compound a small daily gain for 100 years.

It's the packaging that's important. In other words, the narrative that it's wrapped up in.

We remember and respond to narratives. We don't remember or respond to data.

In one psychology study at Stanford, students were told to make a one-minute presentation containing both data and narratives. The audience members were then asked to recall different parts. Only 5% of the audience could recall data, but a full two-thirds recalled the narratives.

Data is boring. It doesn't do anything for most of us. Only when it's framed inside a narrative does the target audience get engaged. If that narrative involves a sob story, that helps a lot (as performers and producers on "America's Got Talent" know – get the audience's emotions involved!).

The problem is, if the narrative teller has stripped out the data or, worse, put in some bad data but spins it inside a really compelling story, we're still likely to be captured by the narrative, potentially investing in (or believing) something that is actually harmful.

Think about that the next time you're pitched an investing idea. Try to get past the narrative and look at the data, if you can. Does the data support the story? If so, maybe it's a good idea. If not, though, maybe it'd be better to pass.

As Danny Kahneman might have said, slow down and let your brain catch up to your emotions. When you do, you're generally more likely to make better decisions.
