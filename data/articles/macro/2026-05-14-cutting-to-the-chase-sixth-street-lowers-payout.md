Cutting to the Chase: Sixth Street Lowers Payout

Don't cry over spilled basis points; the bull case is still intact.

Anthony Schiavone

May 14, 2026, 11:28 PM

Comments

Last week, Sixth Street Speciality Lending (NYSE:TSLX) delivered disappointing news.

The business development company (BDC) cut its quarterly base dividend rate from $0.46 to $0.42 per share. According to CEO Bo Stanley, the dividend reduction “reflects the level of uncertainty we see around near-term activity, including the rate and spread backdrop and also the market volatility caused by geopolitical uncertainty that has occurred since our last call…we have always believed it is appropriate to align our base dividend with the forward earnings power of the business.”

Management also noted that activity-based fees have been relatively muted as credit spreads — particularly for software companies — have widened. When credit spreads rise, companies are less likely to refinance into higher-yielding loans. Fees historically have generated around $0.08 to $0.09 per share in quarterly investment income. In Q1 2026, fees produced just $0.04 per share and it’s unclear when fee income normalizes.

I still think TSLX is one of the higher-quality BDCs, but with hindsight, an initial portfolio allocation of 2.5% instead of 5% would have been more prudent considering the risk-reward/macro setup at the time.

While it’s difficult to find any solace in a dividend cut, here’s my attempt.

On the earnings call, CFO Ian Simmonds said, “we may prove to be moving early on the base dividend adjustment, but our supplemental dividend framework provides the flexibility to capture upside should the activity accelerate.” If fee income and business fundamentals accelerate, management will likely use the supplemental dividend as a lever to return more cash to shareholders.

It also appears management is making this dividend cut to preserve net asset value and financial flexibility. TSLX has a history of being opportunistic and outperforming its BDC peers during periods of market disruption. If this dividend cut provides more financial flexibility to invest incremental capital in a more lender-friendly environment, I think it makes sense. TSLX’s history of NAV growth supports this.

Additionally, the entire BDC sector is being impacted by the same variables. Here’s what TSLX had to say in its stakeholder letter from March:

The forward interest rate curve indicates that the BDC sector is approaching trough earnings.
 
Since the Fed began cutting rates in September 2025, nearly 40% of TSLX’s public peers have cut base dividends, by an average of 20%.
 
We anticipate further downward adjustments across the sector as managers continue to align distributions with lower net investment income levels.

To be clear, the dividend cuts that have started to proliferate across the BDC sector over the past few quarters have nothing to do with credit losses. The loss rates for private credit have been relatively stable. But the math will tell you the sector cannot fund current dividends at the spreads at which it has been deploying capital.

We believe it is important to avoid conflating dividend reductions based on a lower interest rate environment with the broader concept that return on investments within a BDC should drive the ability to meet or exceed its cost of equity.

Given the overwhelming bulk of BDC assets are floating rate in nature,
 
movement in dividend levels over time – both up and down – should not be surprising on its own.
 
Post-COVID, as we moved into a pronounced rate rise cycle, asset level returns increased, leading to higher payout levels through increased dividends. Similarly, as we have seen reference rates decline from the peak last seen in mid-2023, the notion of lowering dividend levels should be in part a logical and expected outcome.

According to TSLX, its 9% dividend cut is lower than the average 20% cut by BDCs peers. A dividend cut is still a dividend cut, but TSLX’s cut appears to be opportunistic rather than a dividend reduction made out of desperation.

Looking forward, I still have concerns about TSLX’s software exposure, but I’m comfortable with that risk for now as credit quality is still strong. TSLX recently extended the final maturity on its revolving credit facility to 2031 with the same terms and pricing conditions, and the stock still trades at a premium to NAV. Both credit and equity investors continue to view TSLX as a quality BDC.
