THE QUESTION

If you could buy only one part of SpaceX — rocket launches, communications satellites, or AI development — which would you choose?

1. It Really Is Rocket Science

By Lou Whiteman
TEAM HIDDEN GEMS

The actual “space” part of SpaceX was a bit of an afterthought in the company’s S-1, overshadowed by higher projected growth areas like AI and Starlink. I have real questions about the long-term outlook for both of those businesses, but the core rocket business has the potential to be a big moneymaker.

We are facing a massive shortage of launch capacity worldwide. Government missions continue to take priority, and Starlink and Amazon (NASDAQ: AMZN) are soaking up whatever capacity remains to launch communications satellites into orbit. Meanwhile, younger companies are pushing for a place in line. There will be demand for upward of 7,000 annual launches by 2030. Last year, there were fewer than 350 orbital space launches worldwide.

There are other companies trying to close that gap, but as Blue Origin reminded us last week, it is hard to reliably launch items into space. Throw in a shortage of launch sites, and there are limits to how fast capacity can grow.

SpaceX is the dominant player in the launch business, but most of its capacity is used in-house. On its own, freed from the obligation to launch Starlink satellites at cost (a scenario that helps pad Starlink’s profitability but eats into space’s results), the launch business would be well positioned to capitalize on that growing demand.

2. None at This Price

By Tim Green
TEAM HIDDEN GEMS

Once I finished laughing at the $28.5 trillion TAM SpaceX claimed in its S-1 filing, I took a deeper look at its businesses. If you ignore price, the launch business certainly has some serious long-term potential, while the Starlink business is harder to like. Buried fiber optic cable can last 50 years, while a Starlink satellite might last five years.

How about the AI business? The company apparently built its Colossus I AI data center in an inefficient way for training its Grok AI models. This multibillion-dollar whoopsie tells you all you need to know about how well the AI business is run. With an expected IPO valuation of $1.75 trillion, the simple answer is “none of the above.”

3. Nothing to See Here

By Seth Jayson
TEAM RULE BREAKERS

This is like asking me to select my favorite treat from the litter box next to the furnace. I don’t like anything I see in there. And no, I don’t care how enthusiastic everyone else might be about the opportunity (as I didn’t share my golden retriever’s enthusiasm for litter box delicacies.)

If you take the time to read the SpaceX registration statement, you’ll see that the best business (Starlink) is at best low-cash profitable, but the future depends on technology that hasn’t been delivered. Space launch is meh and depends on government-funded fairy tales. The AI business is worst-in-class among frontier shops. This is a Beanie Baby, Pokemon, or tulip trade. Don’t pretend otherwise.

4. Picks and Shovels: Terafab

By Tim Beyers
TEAM RULE BREAKERS

I’ll be honest: Space Exploration Technologies at more than 100 times revenue doesn’t interest me. Nevertheless, there are elements of the business that could drive value.

Terafab tops my list.

I’ll understand if that feels odd. Terafab isn’t yet built and will do more than serve SpaceX once it’s live. Tesla (NASDAQ: TSLA), Twitter, and xAI will surely also benefit. That’s fine.

For me, Terafab and its proposed investments in advanced semiconductors and related equipment could prove foundational for building spacefaring data centers. Radiation-resistant chipsets, new zero-G cooling systems, flexible compute that keeps integrity even when the environment it’s operating in is fluid. All that and more is needed, and Terafab could unlock serious value leading development.

5. The Sky Is Not the Limit — It’s the Moat

By Yasser El-Shimy
TEAM RULE BREAKERS

When SpaceX goes public, one division deserves your undivided attention: Starlink. With $11.4 billion in revenue in 2025 (up 50% year over year), an impressive 63% EBITDA margin, and 10.3 million subscribers across 164 countries, Starlink has evolved from a moonshot into a cash machine.

The economics are compelling: Once the constellation is built, each new subscriber costs almost nothing to add. Aviation, maritime, and Starshield (defense) units command premium pricing, while the consumer business solves a genuine civilizational problem — bridging broadband to billions underserved by ground infrastructure. Unlike fiber, Starlink needs no roads, permits, or decades. This is not a telecom; it’s a near-monopoly operating above the clouds. If I could buy only one part of the company, Starlink would be it.
