FAQ: Sell Bonds When Delaying Social Security

The longer you wait to claim Social Security, the more you’re theoretically accumulating a stream of inflation-adjusted, tax-advantaged bonds that will last as long as you do. So, selling the actual bonds in your portfolio -- and holding on to your stocks -- may be the best strategy until you claim your benefit.

Question: When retiring early but not taking Social Security until 70 years old, does that change the recommendation for which investments or savings to take money from first?

Answer: If you wait until age 70 to claim Social Security, you will be among the 10% of retirees who delay as long as possible to get the maximum benefit. However, many experts believe that figure should be much higher.

A study by economists Laurence Kotlikoff, Philip Moeller, and Paul Solman found that more than 90% of American workers aged 45 to 62 should wait until 70 to collect and that the median household forfeits approximately $182,370 in lifetime discretionary spending by claiming too early.

The rationale is clear: For every month you delay, you receive a bigger benefit for the rest of your life -- and your surviving spouse's life if you die first and your benefit was bigger than your spouse's.

That said, there are trade-offs. If you retire before age 70, you'll be withdrawing more from your portfolio while you wait to claim benefits. So, you'll have to be comfortable seeing your portfolio balance go down faster (or go up slower) than if you claimed benefits sooner.

However, once you begin receiving that larger benefit check, you'll rely less on your portfolio than if you had claimed at a younger age.

What to Sell While You Wait

In an interview with retirement expert Dr. Michael Finke for the then-Motley Fool Money podcast (since renamed to Motley Fool Hidden Gems Investing), I asked whether retirees should sell down their stocks or bonds while they wait to claim at age 70.

His response was that one way to think about Social Security is that it's like an inflation-protected annuity constructed from Treasury inflation-protected securities (TIPS). When you delay claiming, you're essentially accumulating more bonds. Therefore, you should sell the bonds in your portfolio while you wait to claim.

In other words, you're exchanging the actual bonds in your portfolio for the theoretical bonds of Social Security. Finke argued that this is an exchange worth making because Social Security is more valuable than bonds; it adjusts annually for inflation and lasts as long as you do, mitigating the risk that you'll outlive your money.

This also means that you should leave your stocks alone as much as possible. As Finke said, "Take the money from your bonds to delay claiming Social Security. Don't touch the allocation to equities. Don't sell your equities to make that bridge. There's no downside. Even if equities go up, you have just as many equities as you did before."

This presumes you have bonds to sell. I know many Motley Fool members have aggressive, stock-heavy portfolios -- even as they enter retirement.

But there's reason to play it safer during the last several years of your career and the first several years of retirement. This time frame has come to be known as the "retirement danger zone" or "retirement risk zone," because it's a time when a bear market could have a disproportionate impact (and a bad one at that) on when you retire and how much you can spend in retirement.

One strategy is to build a "bond tent" to get you through the danger zone. It can also serve as a source of funds while you delay Social Security.

Which Account to Tap

The standard rule of thumb for the order of withdrawals in retirement is to first deplete your taxable brokerage accounts, then your traditional tax-deferred retirement accounts, and finally your Roth accounts.

However, this may not be the optimal sequence in many situations. If you won't claim Social Security until age 70 (or thereabouts), it might make sense to withdraw more from traditional retirement accounts while you delay. That way, you're reducing the values of those accounts quicker, which means your required minimum distributions (RMDs) at age 73 (or 75 if born in 1960 or later) will be smaller.

This is also consistent with the "sell bonds while delaying" strategy, since typical asset location guidance suggests that most types of bonds should be held in traditional retirement accounts.

Meanwhile, you're increasing your Social Security benefit, which has its own tax advantages. At least 15% of the benefit is tax-free, so the bigger the benefit, the more tax-free income it provides.

Of course, everyone's tax situation is unique, so consult a financial planner or tax professional to determine the best withdrawal strategy for you.
