SpaceX's Space Data Centers Face Long Odds

The dream of data centers in space is unlikely to help justify SpaceX's frothy valuation any time soon.

Lou Whiteman

11:59 AM

Comments
10.0

Look to the sky, and the benefit of placing massive infrastructure in orbit is evident: a massive 24/7 energy source that would instantly solve one of the biggest hurdles to the terrestrial data center build-out.

Unseen are the physics of space, which will make Elon Musk and SpaceX's vision for orbital data centers hard to achieve.

Space is a vacuum, which means the rules governing things like conduction and convection, taught as gospel in every high school physics classroom, do not apply. That means servers cannot be cooled using traditional fans or liquid-cooled heat exchangers.

Instead, data centers would rely entirely on thermal radiation, requiring massive, heavy radiator panels larger than anything currently in orbit just to cool a single rack of AI chips.

An orbital data center would also require massive shielding structures to protect chips from space radiation, as well as to insulate against 300-degree Celsius temperature swings as the center moves from direct sunlight to being shadowed by the Earth.

Human ingenuity can solve these problems, but it will take time. Engineers are developing new radiation- and temperature-tolerant microchips, building off of designs currently used in satellites. Future data centers could be deployed in orbits that synchronize with the sun, putting them in near-continuous sunlight to avoid extreme temperature swings.

We also need a launch vehicle capable of taking these heavy items into orbit. SpaceX's Starship hopes to be that vehicle, but it is still in development and has significant launch commitments related to NASA's Artemis project, clogging its schedule for the next few years.

If all goes to plan, the earliest we could realistically see commercially viable data centers in space is the early 2030s. Even that is likely to look more like a server rack in space instead of a full data center. By that time, the economic viability of space-based data centers might depend on scientific advancements in other areas, such as small modular nuclear reactors, cool-running microchips, and more efficient AI, which could make terrestrial options more appealing.

The bottom line for investors is that while space-based data centers are not impossible, they will almost certainly not generate revenue for SpaceX or any other company for years. Those buying into the IPO are being asked to accept a frothy valuation. They should not expect the dream of data centers in space to help justify that valuation any time soon.
