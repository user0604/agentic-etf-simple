THE QUESTION

If you could only check your portfolio once a month, what would you do differently?

1. Would Less Information Lead to Better Gains?

By Rachel Warren
TEAM HIDDEN GEMS

If I only checked my portfolio once a month, I would probably spend a bit less time analyzing price charts and allocate that to even more time studying the underlying health of the businesses I own. I already focus on long-term quality, but this shift would naturally filter out the temptation to react to short-term volatility.

As long-term investors, our conviction must be anchored in the business reality of the stocks we own rather than fickle market sentiment. By severing the daily digital tether, we can allow our compounding machines to operate without the friction of unnecessary interference. This approach makes it easier to ignore the headlines and treat our holdings like silent partnerships in enduring enterprises.

2. Durability Over Thrill

By Neha Chamaria
TEAM HIDDEN GEMS

That’s an easy question for me to answer, because I actually often don’t check my portfolio for weeks at a stretch. When I adopted this approach a couple of years ago, the biggest change was in my own temperament.

I stopped looking for constant validation of my thesis, trying to find a reason behind a stock’s swings even when there was none. Over time, I also traded thrill for durability and sold out of some speculative stocks into “moat” stocks that didn’t require daily reassurance. Because if I can’t monitor daily, my portfolio must be resilient by design. That also meant I don’t try to “play” the market anymore, and focus solely on surviving and beating it with businesses I hold a strong conviction in.

3. Find a Routine and Stick to It

By Alicia Alfiere
TEAM RULE BREAKERS

If I checked my portfolio only once a month, I’d make things as routine as possible and work to remove emotion from my process. I’d make a recurring calendar appointment and make sure that money for new investments already hit my account. Then, I’d consult my investing journal for companies and index funds to buy.

If any stocks were performing poorly, I’d jot them down. Later, I’d consult my journal to review my thesis and to check if I’ve given it enough time to play out. If necessary, I’d spend a few weeks researching if any red flags surfaced.

4. Know What You Own

By Jason Moser
TEAM RULE BREAKERS

I don’t check my portfolio a ton as it is, but it’s definitely more than once a month. I think it would reinforce for me knowing what I own. I’ve made the mistake before of not knowing what I own, all the way back to when my Edward Jones guy had me in mutual funds and tech stocks during the dot-com bubble. I was a novice and didn’t know what I didn’t know. But I’ve made a conscious effort to have a good grasp on what I own, and if I can’t answer those basic questions, I’m OK taking a pass. Like Warren Buffett says, “The stock market is a no-called-strike game. You don’t have to swing at everything — you can wait for your pitch.”

The 2022 growth stock meltdown taught me an important, if rather obvious, lesson: when the market is experiencing large drawdowns, the more often you check your portfolio, the more unhappy you'll be!

These days, I try and make any significant buy/sell decisions on weekends — when the market is closed — when I have plenty of time to think, and without the distractions of real-time market news and up-to-the-minute stock prices. I find that this removes a lot of stress and encourages more patient thinking.
