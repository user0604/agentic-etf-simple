3 Smart Reasons to Do Roth Conversions

Paying taxes today could lead to more after-tax wealth for your retirement and for your heirs.

Robert Brokamp, CFP

May 20, 2026, 12:00 PM

Comments (4)
9.8

As you contributed to IRAs and 401(k)s over your career, you decided how much to save and how to invest those savings.

You also had to make another important decision: When will you pay the taxes?

With traditional retirement accounts, you likely got a tax break on your contributions, but withdrawals will be taxed as ordinary income. With Roth retirement accounts, contributions are not tax deductible, but withdrawals will be tax free (as long as you follow the rules).

This isn't a one-time decision. At any point, regardless of your age or level of income, you can transfer assets from a traditional account into Roth assets in a process known as a conversion. The amount you convert will be added to your taxable income in that year, but then the assets will grow tax free.

Should you pay more taxes today for a lower tax bill in retirement? Here are three scenarios in which this trade-off makes sense.

At a Glance
Your Tax Bracket Will Not Be Significantly Lower in Retirement
Your RMDs Will Be Much More Than You Need
You Want to Leave Your Heirs a Tax-Free Inheritance

The Foolish Bottom Line

1. Your Tax Bracket Will Not Be Significantly Lower in Retirement

Here's the rule of thumb for Roth conversions: If you'll be in a higher tax bracket in retirement, accumulate as much money in Roth accounts as possible. You'll pay higher taxes this year, but you'll get a tax break in a future year when your income is taxed at a higher rate.

That makes perfect sense. But if you'll be in the same or even a lower tax bracket in retirement, having more Roth assets can pay off, according to a report [link downloads a PDF] published by Vanguard last year. It depends on how you'll pay the taxes due to the conversion.

If you pay the taxes by taking money from the retirement account, the conversion usually won't pay off. But paying conversion taxes from a taxable account effectively moves more dollars into a tax-advantaged environment; the less tax efficient that taxable account is, the bigger the benefit.

The Vanguard report introduces the "break-even tax rate" (BETR), which is the future tax rate at which you'd be indifferent between converting and not converting. The key insight is that the BETR can be substantially lower than your current marginal rate, meaning a Roth conversion can make sense even if you expect to be in a lower bracket in the future.

Here's an illustration from the report: Jill is in the 35% bracket now and expects to drop to 24% in retirement, 20 years away. She's considering converting $100,000 from a traditional IRA to a Roth and paying the $35,000 tax bill from her taxable account.

The conventional rule says don't convert because her future rate is lower. But here's the BETR math: The $100,000 triples to $300,000 in the Roth, while the $35,000 she would have kept in her taxable account only doubles to $70,000 after tax drag. Her net conversion outcome is $230,000 ($300,000 minus $70,000 forgone). For the no-conversion path to match that, the future tax rate on $300,000 would need to be 23.3% or lower.

Since her expected future rate of 24% is above that 23.3% BETR, the conversion is actually the better move.

Vanguard provides a calculator you can use to determine your breakeven tax rate and estimate whether a conversion is worth considering, depending on whether the money to pay the conversion taxes comes from cash, a tax-efficient brokerage account, a tax-inefficient brokerage account, or the retirement account itself.

2. Your RMDs Will Be Much More Than You Need

When you reach age 73 (or 75 if born in 1960 or later), Uncle Sam will expect you to make annual withdrawals from your traditional retirement accounts via required minimum distributions (RMDs). The amount you were supposed to withdraw but didn't could be subject to a 10% or 25% penalty.

Roth accounts, however, are free from RMDs. You can leave the money in the account for as long as you like. (There's one exception, which is addressed in No. 3 below.)

For most Americans, the majority of their retirement savings is in traditional accounts. According to the Investment Company Institute, of the $18.3 trillion in IRAs at the end of 2025, only 13% was in Roth accounts. So RMDs for many Americans will be substantial.

The actual amount required to be withdrawn depends on the beneficiary of the account and, if it's a spouse, her or his age. Most account owners use Table III of the Uniform Lifetime Table from IRS Publication 590-B. Percentage-wise, here's how much an account owner would be required to withdraw at various ages using Table III.

RMD Percentages Based on Table III Distribution Periods

Age	73	75	80	85	90	95	100
RMD %	3.77%	4.07%	4.95%	6.25%	8.2%	11.24%	15.63%

In the first few years, RMDs likely won't be burdensome. But the percentages get bigger rather quickly, forcing account owners to withdraw more than they need -- which will also increase their tax bills and perhaps increase their Medicare premiums due to income-related monthly adjustment amount (IRMAA) surcharges.

The potential remedy: Convert money in traditional accounts to Roth accounts before RMD age.

3. You Want to Leave Your Heirs a Tax-Free Inheritance

The rules governing inherited retirement accounts have become more complicated over the past several years. The beneficiary of an account may or may not have to take annual distributions, and the account may be required to be completely depleted within 5 years, 10 years, or not as long as the beneficiary is alive. It depends on the relationship between the deceased and the beneficiary, the age of the deceased, the age of the beneficiary, and the difference between the ages. (Learn more by reading "Estate Planning and Retirement Accounts.")

A common scenario is that retirement account owners want to leave those accounts to kids, grandkids, or anyone else more than 10 years younger. In most of those situations, the account must be emptied by the 10th year after the original owner's death. This applies to both traditional and Roth inherited accounts.

In the case of a traditional account, this could force the beneficiary to make withdrawals that will increase their taxable income -- perhaps at a time when they're in their peak earning years. However, in the case of an inherited Roth account, the required withdrawals will be tax free.

The Foolish Bottom Line

No decision about taxes should be taken in isolation. A Roth conversion will increase your adjusted gross income (AGI), which could have an impact on other aspects of your finances, including:

Eligibility for various tax deductions and credits
The amount of Social Security that is included in taxable income
College financial aid packages
The amount paid for Affordable Care Act or Medicare premiums

Make sure you do additional research and perhaps seek the advice of a financial planner or tax professional to determine whether a Roth conversion makes sense for you.
