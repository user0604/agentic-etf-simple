My “Young Buffett” Japan Experiment

How would a young Warren Buffet view the current net-net prospects in Japan's public market?

Joshua Simka

Jun 1, 2026, 10:30 AM

Comments (1)
7.3

There's a version of Warren Buffett most investors forget about. I'm not talking about the Berkshire Hathaway (NYSE: BRKB) sage sipping Cherry Coke and buying Burlington Northern. I mean the young Buffett, who would buy any business trading at $0.50 on the dollar just because the math worked.

That's the investor I was trying to channel when, in December, I decided to assemble a basket of Japanese "net-nets." The term -- from Buffett's teacher and mentor, Benjamin Graham -- refers to stocks so cheap that even if the business were to go bust tomorrow, you'd still come out ahead on the liquidation value alone. Just take a company's current assets (cash, inventory, receivables) and subtract all liabilities. That double subtraction is where the "net net" comes from: net current assets, net of total liabilities.

One disclaimer up front: This was a winter-break thought experiment, not a live portfolio; I didn't actually buy any of these stocks. But I screened for and researched them as if I were going to. That turned out to be half the fun. Not so fun? My sense of regret. Because as you'll see shortly, I wish I had bought.

Why Japan and Why Now?

Here's a stat that should make any serious investor sit up: According to the Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE), 43% of Japanese companies trade below book value. In the U.S., that figure is roughly 5%. This structural anomaly has been hiding in plain sight for decades.

Historically, Japan's corporate culture rewarded stability over returns. Companies hoarded cash, kept cozy cross-shareholding relationships with business partners, and generally ignored the concept of "cost of capital," the minimum return a company needs to generate to justify its investors' money. But that's changing.

The TSE has begun nudging -- and occasionally shoving -- companies into action. In 2023, it told every Prime and Standard market issuer trading below book to publish a capital efficiency plan or explain why it hadn't. By mid-2025, according to Japan's Financial Services Agency, 92% of Prime Market companies had complied. Name-and-shame lists went up, activists arrived, and record dividends -- roughly $127 billion in fiscal 2025, per Trustnet -- started flowing out. A report from global asset manager Janus Henderson noted that 2025 marked the year Japan's governance reforms moved "from being a compliance exercise to tangible value creation."

That backdrop is what made the thesis possible: Find small Japanese companies trading well below their book value, often for no more than $0.70 on the dollar, sitting on mountains of cash, with operating businesses the market was effectively valuing at zero. The reforms meant there was finally a credible answer to the value investor's most dangerous question: What's the catalyst?

The Screening Process

I ran filters in YCharts looking for the classic net-net profile, Japan edition. It looks something like this:

Market cap: Between 5 billion yen and 150 billion yen (small enough to be ignored but big enough to have real filings)
Price-to-book value: Less than or equal to 0.8x
Net cash: Approximately 30% to 50% of market cap (the "heads, I win; tails, I don't lose much" part)
EV/EBIT: Between 1.0x and 3.0x (meaning the market was pricing the business at roughly zero)
Basic profitability: No so-called melting ice cubes and no perpetual cash incinerators

What came back was a list of wonderfully boring companies I'd never heard of. Niche cable makers. Sewing machine manufacturers. Industrial valve suppliers. Regional breweries. The kind of businesses that don't show up in anyone's Reddit (NYSE: RDDT) feed.

For filings, I leaned on the companies' investor relations sites. Don't speak Japanese? Me neither. Browser translation handles the basics surprisingly well, and for trickier sections, I'd paste text into ChatGPT or ask Claude to summarize what a segment breakdown actually meant. AI tools were genuinely useful here to clear the language barrier fast.

The Basket

I landed on eight companies across industrials, materials, and consumer businesses. Here's a little tour. (A quick note: Japanese stocks trade on the TSE using numeric codes rather than the letter-based ticker symbols American investors are used to. Think of them as the Japanese equivalent of AAPL or GOOG.)

It's worth mentioning that if I were actually going in, I'd want to buy a diversified basket rather than concentrating in one or two. The whole point of the net-net approach is that you're playing the odds across many cheap stocks, not betting the farm on any single one.

I'd also keep in mind that Japanese shares typically trade in round lots of 100, which can make diversifying a challenge, and depending on your broker, international trades can come with additional fees worth factoring into your return math. (At the time of writing, Nakakita Seisakusho, for instance, trades for 5,170 yen -- about $32.44 -- per share. That means that before trading and currency exchange fees, minimum capital required to enter that position is 517,000 yen, or about $3,243.)

Hi-Lex is an automotive control-cable and door closure-system supplier. One of the least-expensive on P/B alone, already unwinding cross-shareholdings and buying back stock. But it is, so far, the worst performer of the group at -15%.

Canare Electric

https://www.canare.co.jp/ir/

Cable and connector maker. Extremely cheap at around 0.5x book with a massive cash cushion of around 9.3 billion yen at the FY2025 year-end.

JUKI

https://www.juki.co.jp/en/ir/index.html

Industrial sewing machines and related equipment; caught in a sector downturn. Cyclical recovery plus TSE governance pressure have so far pushed it almost 20% higher.

Nakakita Seisakusho

https://www.nakakita-s.co.jp/ir

Nakakita Manufacturing Works (in English) makes industrial valves and remote-control systems. In its financial results for the third quarter of the fiscal year ending May 2026, Nakakita reported revenue for the quarter up 32.1%, operating profit up 63.4%, with revenue growing nearly twice as fast as SG&A, showing operating leverage. Ordinary profit was up 97.7% to 1,686 million yen.

Bando Chemical

https://www.bandogrp.com/eng/ir/index.html

Industrial and automotive belts with a steadily improving capital-allocation story. Slow, steady, boring -- and I mean that as a compliment! The company modified its shareholder-return policy, executed a 2 billion-yen buyback program in FY2025, authorized another 2 billion-yen repurchase this month, and raised the annual dividend from 76 yen to 120 yen.

DKK

https://denkikogyo.co.jp/en/ir/

Telecom antennas, telecom facilities, and induction-heating equipment. The company has a history of accounting/control problems and recently changed auditors, making it one of the riskier companies in this lineup. Fundnote/Kaihou and Oasis Management have applied activist pressure, and the company's IR news feed includes multiple corrections to financial disclosures.

Daiichi Jitsugyo

https://www.djk.co.jp/en/ir/

This is a general machinery trading company which, under new leadership, has adopted a clearer shareholder-return framework, as part of which a buyback was launched this month. The stock is up 7%.

Unitika

https://www.unitika.co.jp/e/ir/

And then there's the poster child.

Textiles and materials. Unitika has been a 5.5-bagger since mid-December. Management has launched a sweeping restructuring. The business revitalization plan is centered on withdrawal from unprofitable businesses, cost reduction, high-value-added product expansion, and organizational strengthening.

How Did the Basket Actually Do?

In short, it's much too soon to tell. As with any basket of stocks -- notional or backed by real money -- you want to allow at least three to five years for the thesis to play out. But if we must peek early, it all honestly depends on just a single stock.

Assuming equal positions across all eight companies, the Young Buffett Basket returned roughly 62% in total, which sounds great until you look under the hood. Unitika surged 453%, and without it, the remaining seven positions averaged a gain of 6.31%.

A couple honest caveats worth naming.

The 62% figure assumes simple equal weighting. As mentioned above, position sizing matters here and, given the 100-share round lot requirements, could make this type of basket inaccessible to some investors.
I must emphasize the importance of the basket approach. Net-net investing isn't about finding the one; it's about buying enough cheap, asset-protected companies that the winners more than cover the losers. In this case, one extraordinary outlier has -- so far, at least -- done exactly that.
The Deeper Lesson

The thesis held up where governance reform and corporate action were most decisive. Unitika's management actually did what they said they'd do. Those that have lagged are mostly still waiting for their catalyst.

Risks are real. These are illiquid small caps. Currency moves can swamp the thesis. And Japan's government recently floated rules that could raise the threshold for shareholder proposals. If passed, these could slow the activism engine. Especially in Japan, governance progress is not a straight line.

But the downside is cushioned by design. Every stock in this basket had net cash as a floor. If the business goes nowhere, you're still holding a company worth more dead than alive.

Japan's market remains cheap by global standards. Reform momentum is real. And the playbook -- screen for extreme cheapness, scour the filings, and wait for a catalyst -- is something any investor with patience and a browser translator can run.

I think the young Buffett would have loved this hunting ground.
