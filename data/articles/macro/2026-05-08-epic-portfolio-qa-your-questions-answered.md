Q: 5 of these are rated F by Schwab (AXON, BWXT, MRVL, NU, RKLB) and 6 are D (DASH, MRNA, RDDT, SNDR, TSLA, TKO). Is that a reason for concern?

A: Thanks for the question, fellow Fool. Different opinions make a market, so we're not overly concerned when our outlook conflicts with another popular source of investment research whose goals or time horizon may differ from ours.

Schwab Equity Ratings® are promoted as 12-month outlooks, and I wouldn't ignore reading the analysis qualifying each of those ratings -- just as we encourage members to do when following our recommendations. While Charles Schwab (NYSE: SCHW) no longer relies on the trading commissions it helped to eliminate, the business largely makes money today generating interest on idle cash in brokerage accounts.

Either way, that rating system's incentive is (still) to help identify potential positions to enter with a near-term exit implied. We'd rather follow a philosophy of owning positions longer and managing cash so that planned actions (or surprise reactions) don't require selling an eventual winner too early. -- Loren Horst

Q: I want to invest in only 30 stocks. How should I go about selection? e.g. Eliminate stocks with lower potential return, keep same % of cautious, aggressive, moderate?

A: Honestly, the best answer here depends entirely on factors only you know -- your other holdings, your time horizon, your risk tolerance, and your liquidity needs. We can't make that call for you, and we'd be cautious about anyone who claims they can.

That said, your instinct to maintain the balance of cautious, moderate, and aggressive positions is a sound one. The diversification across those three tiers is a core feature of how Epic Portfolio is built -- concentrating too heavily in aggressive names increases volatility, while skewing too cautious might limit your long-term upside.

Our broader encouragement: The more stocks you own, the better your odds of holding the eventual big winners without knowing in advance which ones they'll be. If 30 is your number, that's a perfectly reasonable portfolio -- just go in with eyes open that you're making active choices to exclude some companies we believe in. Whatever you decide, the goal remains the same: a broadly diversified collection of great businesses, held for the long term.

No single stock -- no matter how compelling -- should put your sleep at risk.

Q: In addition to the risks that you mention in the Risk Report, aren't there specific internal company risks related to Strategy / Tactics / Execution?

A: Yes, absolutely. The Risk Report focuses on broader portfolio-level and macro risks, but every company carries its own internal risk profile. These can include things like:

Strategy risk: Is the company pursuing the right markets? Is its long-term vision sound, or is it chasing trends?
Execution risk: Can management actually deliver on its roadmap? Are key milestones being hit?
Leadership risk: Is there key-person dependency? Are there signs of management instability or misaligned incentives?
Competitive positioning risk: Is the company's moat widening or eroding? Are new entrants threatening its core business?
Capital allocation risk: Is the company investing wisely or burning cash on low-return initiatives?
Product/technology risk: Is the product roadmap keeping pace with the market? Is there technical debt or development risk?

The good news: We think about these questions deeply for every stock in the Epic Portfolio. Each recommendation write-up includes a "What Are the Risks?" section specifically designed to surface the most important company-level risks we see. We'd encourage you to revisit that section for any position that's giving you concern. It won't answer every question, but it's the clearest window into how we're thinking about downside at the individual company level.

Q: WING -- any luck in the future?

A: Wingstop (NASDAQ: WING) remains a recommendation across Motley Fool services and hasn't been sold -- which we think is worth noting up front. The long-term chart is a thing of beauty; the shorter-term picture is a different story.

Our own Rick Munarriz captures the tension well: Wingstop came out of the pandemic as a rock star, as one of the few restaurant concepts posting positive comps while dining rooms were closed, thanks to its naturally takeout-friendly wing business. But it's hit a rough patch. Same-store sales were down nearly 9% last quarter, and while bad quarters happen, a company hitting a couple in a row demands attention.

The silver lining is the franchise model. As Rick points out, Wingstop doesn't carry the same margin pressure that company-owned concepts are absorbing right now; franchisees bear more of that burden. And the unit economics that made Wingstop franchises so attractive -- small footprint, cheap to open, high demand -- haven't fundamentally changed.

The question is whether the brand can turn comps around soon enough to justify patience. Team Rule Breakers thinks the long-term story remains intact, but this one requires conviction and a stomach for near-term turbulence. It's best owned inside a diversified portfolio.

Q: Should Palantir (PLTR) be bought prior to May earnings?

A: We're actually answering this one after earnings, but let's quickly close the loop: Palantir (NASDAQ: PLTR) crushed Q1 expectations, reporting revenue of $1.63 billion (up 85% year over year) and EPS of $0.33, well ahead of the $0.28 consensus. Net income quadrupled year over year, and the company raised its full-year guidance to $7.65 billion. A genuinely spectacular quarter... And yet the stock still fell nearly 7% following earnings, largely on valuation concerns (Palantir trades for something around 150x earnings).

Which actually proves the broader point perfectly: This is why we don't rely on earnings. Even when a company delivers one of its best quarters ever, the stock can go the wrong way in the short term. As long-term investors, we're not trying to catch a 5% or 10% move around a single report; we're trying to own compounding businesses for years and decades. In that context, the day-to-day movements around earnings can be nothing more than...noise. Buy great businesses in a well-diversified portfolio, and let time do the work.

The goal isn't to find the one perfect stock. It's to own a collection of great businesses and let time and compounding do the heavy lifting.

Q: Do you want us to reinvest the dividends that some of the stocks pay or keep them as cash?

A: In our real-money portfolio, we're taking dividends as cash. That's partly for clean reporting purposes and is how our brokerage account is set up. That cash then gets redeployed in future allocation rounds, which gives us some flexibility to put it where we see the best opportunities.

That said, for most individual investors, reinvesting dividends automatically is a perfectly solid default, especially if you don't need the income. Compounding those payments back into the same positions over years and decades adds up more than most people expect.

If you don't need the cash, you could choose to reinvest. But if you'd rather mirror what we're doing or maintain flexibility, taking dividends as cash and deploying them intentionally works just as well. Either way, you're not making a mistake -- it's one of those decisions in which both answers are pretty good.

Q: Should we assume SpaceX, Open AI, and Anthropic will be in the major index this year?

A: SpaceX is the most certain of the three – and the indexes are actively changing their rules to accommodate it. S&P Dow Jones Indices is considering a significant rule change that would allow recent IPOs to be admitted to the S&P 500 much sooner, applying to mega-cap stocks with a market cap of at least $200 billion. Under the proposed changes, a company would only need to be public for six months (down from 12), with profitability and minimum float requirements waived for mega-caps. The proposed changes are open for comment until May 28, with implementation potentially as early as June 8 – not coincidentally, just before SpaceX's expected late June IPO.

As for OpenAI and Anthropic – maybe, if they go public this year. Anthropic is in talks to raise capital at a $900 billion valuation ahead of a public offering contemplated for later this year, while OpenAI was recently valued at $852 billion ahead of a planned IPO. Any of these companies would instantly be among the largest publicly traded in the United States upon listing. Bottom line: SpaceX index inclusion looks increasingly likely by year-end. OpenAI and Anthropic are possible but not certain.
