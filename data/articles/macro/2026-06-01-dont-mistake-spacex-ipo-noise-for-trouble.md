Don't Mistake SpaceX IPO Noise for Trouble

I think the reported chaos is overdone, and I still expect a successful IPO.

Lou Whiteman

Jun 1, 2026, 9:57 AM

Comments

Is the Space Exploration Technologies (SpaceX) IPO in trouble?

Late last week, the company reportedly lowered expectations for the largest IPO in history, lowering its targeted valuation to $1.8 trillion from $2 trillion. The new target follows a week of news flow, mostly gasping (and sometimes giggling) at projections contained within the filing, including a total addressable market approximately equal to U.S. GDP.

Large investors have also flagged an apparent tension between AI and space-related activities within the company and questioned whether there will be enough cash to fund the billions in capex required by each side.

I share many of those concerns and don't anticipate personally buying shares when SpaceX goes public. But I think the reported chaos is overdone, and I still expect a successful IPO. I think a lot of the turbulence right now stems from how SpaceX and Elon Musk allocated shares in the offering.

SpaceX is planning a retail-friendly offering, allocating up to 30% of the shares to mom-and-pop investors. That's three times more than a typical large offering. We can celebrate giving retail a bigger taste as a way to democratize finance. But it also creates nightmares for the bankers running the offering.

The primary job of the bankers underwriting an IPO is to "discover" the right price for the offering by building a book of demand. That's relatively straightforward when working with large institutional investors and fund managers. Bankers accept large, formal indications of interest stating how much stock each big fund wants and at what price.

Retail is more fragmented and buying in smaller amounts. Instead of dozens of large block requests, the bankers must sort through thousands of bids through dozens of brokerage apps. Each bid can be pulled at any time.

It's a herding cats problem: Working with thousands of individuals who are each interested in just a handful of shares is simply a lot harder than working with dozens of funds you already have a relationship with and who will each submit orders in millions or billions of dollars.

Obviously, the bankers are paid very well for their work. I don't mean this to say poor bankers. But the setup also makes me take any reports of weakness or concerns about the IPO with a grain of salt. SpaceX's path to the public markets is as complicated as its plan to reach Mars. It's not set up to provide the clarity market watchers love to see.

Don't take that uncertainty as a sign the IPO is in trouble.
