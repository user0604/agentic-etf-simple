Social Security for Spouses, Exes, and Widow(er)s

The rules about claiming Social Security benefits based on someone else’s work record can be confounding. Here’s what you need to know.

Robert Brokamp, CFP

10:15 AM

Comments
10.0

The formula that determines the Social Security benefit you'll receive based on your own work record is not complicated. A primary insurance amount (PIA) is calculated from your 35 highest-earning working years, adjusted for inflation; that's the amount you'll receive if you claim at your full retirement age (FRA), which is 67 if you were born in 1960 or later.

You'll receive less or more than your PIA if you claim at a different age. You can claim as early as 62 or as late as 70. The longer you wait, the bigger the benefit.

However, if you are or were married, you may receive benefits based on your spouse's, ex-spouse's, or deceased spouse's record. These formulas are much more convoluted, and they're the ones about which I've seen the most confusion about Social Security. In this article, I cover the essential facts you need to know.

1. Current Spouses

The basic rules. If you file for benefits and your spouse hasn't yet filed, then your benefit will be based on your own work record. However, if your spouse has filed, then you may be eligible for a spousal benefit if it's larger.

The maximum spousal benefit is 50% of the worker-spouse's primary insurance amount -- that is, the amount the worker would receive at their FRA, not what they're actually collecting.

A spouse can claim a spousal benefit as early as age 62, provided the worker-spouse has already filed for their own retirement benefit.

Does delaying help? Up to a point, yes -- but only up to your FRA. There is no increase for waiting beyond that age.

Switching benefits. Under what is known as "deemed filing," anyone who files for spousal benefits is also deemed to be filing for their own retirement benefit. You'll receive your own benefit plus any excess spousal benefit if the spousal benefit is larger.

Working while receiving benefits. If you claim a spousal benefit before your full retirement age and continue to work, the retirement earnings test applies. The thresholds and withholding rates are the same as for retirement benefits.

For beneficiaries younger than full retirement age throughout the year in 2026, the annual earnings limit is $24,480; $1 in benefits will be withheld for every $2 earned above this limit. For beneficiaries reaching FRA in 2026, the annual earnings limit is $65,160; $1 in benefits will be withheld for every $3 earned above this limit until the month the beneficiary reaches FRA. At FRA and beyond, the earnings test no longer applies.

If the higher-earning spouse is below FRA and earns above the limit, both their own benefit and the spousal benefits paid on their record can be withheld. However, this is not true for someone receiving spousal benefits; working before FRA will reduce their benefits but not the other spouse's.

Those benefits aren't lost forever. Once FRA is reached, the Social Security Administration recalculates the monthly benefit to account for previously withheld amounts, resulting in an increased monthly benefit.

Essential Facts

Claiming early hits spousal benefits harder than retirement benefits. Claiming standard benefits before your full retirement age reduces them by 5/9th of 1% monthly for the first 36 months. Claiming spousal benefits early reduces them by 25/36th of 1% for the first 36 months -- a steeper reduction. However, after the first 36 months, the relative size of the reduction flips (spousal benefits aren't reduced as much as retirement benefits). The combined effect is a larger overall reduction if claimed at age 62: up to 35% for spousal benefits versus 30% for benefits based on your own record at the same age.
Marriage duration. You generally need to have been married at least one year before claiming spousal benefits (with exceptions for the parent of the worker's child).
2. Divorced Spouses

The basic rules. You may claim on an ex-spouse's record if you meet all of the following criteria:

The marriage lasted at least 10 years.
You are currently unmarried.
You are at least 62 years old.
Your ex is entitled to Social Security retirement or disability benefits.

Like a current spouse, you can receive up to 50% of the ex's PIA while the ex is alive.

The independently entitled rule. If you have been divorced for at least two years and your ex-spouse is at least 62, you can claim benefits even if your ex hasn't filed. This rule was specifically designed to prevent an ex-spouse from blocking benefits by refusing to file.

Does delaying help? Switching benefits? Same rules as current spouses: Spousal benefits max out at FRA -- no delayed credits -- and you'll receive a benefit based on your work record or your spouse's record, whichever is higher.

Working while receiving benefits. The earnings test applies to divorced-spouse benefits exactly as it does to current-spouse benefits. However, your ex's earnings have no effect on your benefit. Only your own earnings matter for the test.

Essential Facts

Your claim doesn't affect your ex's benefit. The divorced-spouse benefit is paid from Social Security's general trust fund, not deducted from your ex-spouse's payment. Their monthly check remains exactly the same whether you claim or not. Also, the Social Security Administration doesn't notify the ex.
Your ex's remarriage doesn't affect your benefit. Your ex-spouse's current marital status has no effect on your eligibility for divorced-spouse benefits. The new spouse can also claim spousal benefits. Both the ex and current spouse can claim simultaneously, and neither claim diminishes the other.
Multiple ex-spouses on one record. Social Security allows unlimited divorced-spouse beneficiaries on one worker's record. Each person's benefit is calculated independently, and none affects the others.
Your own remarriage generally ends eligibility, though there's an important exception we'll address in the survivor section below.
You don't need your ex's Social Security number. The Social Security Administration can locate the record with a name, date of birth, and other identifying information.
3. Widows and Widowers

The basic rules. A surviving spouse can claim as early as age 60 (50 if disabled, or at any age if caring for the deceased's child under age 16 or a disabled child). The marriage generally must have lasted at least nine months, with exceptions for accidental death and line-of-duty military death. The maximum benefit is 100% of what the deceased was receiving (or would have been entitled to), not 50%. Survivor benefits are generally more generous than living-spouse benefits.

Does delaying help? Yes, up to the survivor FRA (which can differ slightly from retirement FRA for those born between 1945 and 1961). Survivor payment amounts start at 71.5% at age 60 and can rise to 100% at survivor full retirement age. There are no delayed credits on survivor benefits past survivor FRA.

Switching benefits. Deemed filing applies to retirement and spousal benefits but not to survivor benefits. Widows and widowers can file a restricted application to claim only one type of benefit (their own or the survivor benefit) and later switch to the other. This option is unique to survivor benefits and unavailable for spousal benefits.

In practical terms, a widow or widower can claim a reduced survivor benefit at 60, let their own retirement benefit grow with delayed credits until 70, and then switch. Or, if their own benefit at 70 will be smaller than the survivor benefit at survivor FRA, they can claim their own benefit at 62 and switch to the full survivor benefit later.

Working while receiving benefits. If a survivor claims before FRA and continues to work, the earnings test applies. One technical point: Although FRA for survivor benefits may be earlier, the SSA uses the FRA for retirement benefits for the earnings test.

This has direct implications for the survivor-then-switch strategy described earlier. If you're planning to claim a reduced survivor benefit at 60 and let your own retirement benefit grow until 70, that bridge income only materializes if your earnings are below the threshold. The strategy works best for survivors who have actually stopped working or significantly reduced their hours.

Also, note that disabled-widow(er) benefits (available as early as age 50) are not subject to the retirement earnings test but instead follow disability rules.

Essential Facts

Remarriage rules. If you remarry before age 60, you will lose eligibility for survivor benefits. However, if you remarry after age 60, you can still claim your deceased spouse's survivor benefit. If the surviving spouse remarries before age 60 but the marriage ends due to death, divorce, or annulment, Social Security will restore eligibility for survivor benefits.
The higher earner's delay credits pass through. If a worker delayed claiming Social Security, those higher benefits can raise a surviving-spouse or surviving-divorced-spouse benefit. In other words, when the higher earner waits longer, the widow or widower can sometimes inherit a larger monthly check. This is one of the most compelling reasons for the higher earner in a couple to delay until age 70, especially if it's likely that the higher earner will die before the other spouse.
The widow's limit, often referred to as the RIB-LIM (retirement insurance benefit limitation). If your deceased spouse filed early, you'll be forever limited to either the amount they were drawing or 82.5% of their full-retirement-age benefit, whichever is higher.
The $255 lump-sum death payment. You will be eligible for a one-time payment of $255 when your spouse passes away. You have up to two years to claim the benefit.
Divorced surviving spouses. If you were married for at least 10 years and your ex dies, you may claim a survivor benefit on their record, and your own remarriage after age 60 does not disqualify you.
