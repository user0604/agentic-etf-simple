This week, the confidential prospectus SpaceX filed with the SEC in April could finally become publicly accessible. A mid-June IPO IPO date could follow shortly thereafter.

Last month, NYU finance professor Aswath Damodaran -- the dean of valuation as far as I'm concerned -- published a model in which he valued Space X at roughly $1.22 trillion. (Read it here.)

You can decide whether or not that's fair. What's irrefutable is that a lot of soon-to-be IPO'd companies aren't just valued on future cash flows discounted back to their present value.

These companies are valued primarily on the promise of expansions in their businesses that have yet to materialize. We call this optionality, and we use it all the time in Rule Breakers.

When we do, we tend to ask three questions:

How likely is the expansion?
How big is the target market?
What does it cost to enter?

I can tell you from experience that the third question is the most vital, and the easiest to get wrong.

If you're among the many to dig into SpaceX when it comes out, look at the margins history and what it has historically cost SpaceX to expand.

With satellite internet and data centers in space (among other things), the options for SpaceX to expand quickly are surely vast. The cost to get that expansion may be equally vast.
