SpaceX IPO: Your Cheat Sheet

The Motley Fool Team

Jun 2, 2026, 12:00 PM

Comments (3)
9.7
Key Takeaways
SpaceX plans to list on Nasdaq under ticker SPCX around June 12 at a targeted valuation near $1.75 trillion, which would be the largest IPO ever.
SpaceX runs three businesses under one roof: rocket launches, Starlink satellite internet, and the xAI division that includes Grok and social media platform X.
Starlink is highly profitable, but the newly acquired xAI division is burning enough cash to push the whole company to a multibillion-dollar annual net loss.
The Business

Elon Musk founded SpaceX in 2002 with the goal of making space travel cheap enough to eventually colonize Mars. The company spent its first decade building reusable rockets that could land back on Earth after launch, which dropped the cost of getting payloads into orbit from billions of dollars per mission to tens of millions. That cost advantage is what made the rest of the company possible.

In 2020, SpaceX began launching Starlink, a network of small satellites that beam internet down to ground terminals anywhere on Earth. And in February 2026, SpaceX absorbed Musk's separate AI start-up, xAI, which brought the Grok chatbot, the social media platform X (formerly Twitter), and a Memphis-based data center called Colossus under the SpaceX umbrella.

Musk remains CEO and chairman.

SpaceX generated $18.7 billion in revenue in 2025, up 33% from $14.1 billion in 2024. Here are its three segments:

Starlink is the engine. It brought in 61% of 2025 revenue, grew almost 50% year over year, and was the only segment that turned a profit. The Space segment lost money because SpaceX spent more than $3 billion developing Starship, its next-generation rocket. The AI segment lost the most because training frontier models like Grok requires enormous spending on chips, electricity, and buildings to house them.

The reusable rocket business is what gives SpaceX a cost advantage no competitor has matched, and it is the foundation the rest of the company is built on. Since 2023, SpaceX has launched more than 80% of all payload mass sent to orbit worldwide with a 99% mission success rate, and even Amazon (NASDAQ: AMZN) has paid SpaceX to launch satellites for its competing internet constellation Project Kuiper because no one else can deliver them as cheaply.

Green Flags
Starlink got more profitable as it grew in 2025. Revenue rose 50% to $11.4 billion while operating income rose 120% to $4.4 billion, meaning costs grew more slowly than revenue. And its 63% cash-flow margin beats almost every traditional internet provider.
Starlink doubled its subscribers in 12 months. The service went from 5 million subscribers at the end of Q1 2025 to 10.3 million by the end of Q1 2026, and growth has room to keep running given that hundreds of millions of rural and remote households globally still have no terrestrial broadband option that cable or fiber companies can profitably build to.
No competitor is close in launches. SpaceX has launched more than 80% of global payload mass since 2023 with a 99% success rate, and Amazon's Kuiper, the most-funded challenger, has roughly 300 production satellites in orbit versus Starlink's 9,600.
Starlink's customer base is spread across 10.3 million subscribers in 164 countries plus enterprise and government contracts, so no single customer concentration risk dominates the Connectivity segment.
No insiders are selling shares at the IPO. All shares offered come from SpaceX itself raising fresh capital, and proceeds are earmarked for Starship development, Starlink expansion, and AI infrastructure rather than debt repayment or insider cash-outs.
Red Flags / Areas to Watch
Musk controls the company outright. He will hold about 85% of voting power on 42% of the equity through Class B shares that get 10 votes each, and SpaceX's "controlled company" election removes Nasdaq requirements for a majority-independent board and independent compensation and nominating committees.
Losses are mounting. SpaceX lost $4.9 billion in 2025 and $4.28 billion in just Q1 2026, with accumulated losses since inception now $41.3 billion. The AI segment alone is burning roughly $2.5 billion per quarter.
Musk-controlled companies do hundreds of millions of dollars in business with one another. SpaceX and xAI together paid Tesla (NASDAQ: TSLA) roughly $650 million in 2025 for Megapack batteries, vehicles, and other goods, and the S-1 acknowledges Musk "does not devote his full time and attention" to SpaceX given his roles at Tesla, Neuralink, and The Boring Company.
A Tesla-SpaceX merger may be on the table. CNBC reported in late May that Musk has discussed folding Tesla and SpaceX into a single entity, and the amended S-1 added language saying SpaceX "may issue a significant amount of equity in connection with future transactions," which markets are reading as a merger signal. A combined entity would be worth roughly $3.4 trillion and sit entirely under Musk's voting control.
Starship, the rocket that anchors SpaceX's growth plans, is still failing tests. Starship is a next-generation rocket designed to carry far heavier cargo into space than the rockets SpaceX uses today, and the company needs it working to launch the next round of Starlink satellites and the orbital data centers it plans to begin deploying in 2028.
A big chunk of revenue comes from the U.S. government, and Musk's politics put it at risk. SpaceX holds about $9 billion in active NASA contracts, $5.9 billion in Pentagon launch contracts, plus classified work for the National Reconnaissance Office. Musk has feuded publicly with the Trump administration, which has, at times, threatened to cancel federal contracts with his companies (outside the S-1), and the S-1 also warns that the Grok chatbot could draw its own regulatory scrutiny.
Bottom Line

SpaceX is two cash-generating businesses (rockets and Starlink, with the rocket segment's reported loss driven entirely by Starship R&D) attached to a third (xAI) that is losing $2.5 billion per quarter, all wrapped in a governance structure that gives Musk near-total control. The biggest reason to take the IPO seriously is Starlink's near-monopoly economics; the biggest reason for caution is that its $1.75 trillion market cap prices in successful execution on Starship, orbital AI data centers, and Grok, none of which are working at full scale yet.
