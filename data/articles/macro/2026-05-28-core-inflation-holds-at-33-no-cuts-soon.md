Core Inflation Holds at 3.3%, No Cuts Soon

Monthly price gains slowed from March's spike, but yearly inflation is still running well above the Fed's 2% goal. That keeps rate cuts out of reach.

The Motley Fool Team

May 28, 2026, 9:27 AM

Comments
9.0

Snapshot

The Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the PCE price index, rose 0.4% in April and 3.8% over the past year. Economists had expected a 0.5% monthly gain and a 3.8% annual rate, so the monthly read came in softer than feared.
Core PCE, which strips out food and energy, climbed 0.2% on the month and 3.3% year-over-year. The annual figure matched forecasts, while the monthly pace ran below the 0.3% economists penciled in.
Goods prices jumped 0.7% as gasoline surged 5.5%. Services prices rose 0.3%, with housing and utilities up 0.6% and food services and accommodations up 0.5%. Housing alone posted its biggest monthly gain since at least January 2025.
Personal income was flat against forecasts for a 0.4% increase, dragged down by a drop in farm proprietors' income after a federal aid program closed. Real disposable income fell 0.5%, and the saving rate slipped to 2.6%.

Analysis & implications

The monthly numbers came in cooler than expected on both headline and core, which offers some relief after March's hot 0.7% jump. But the annual readings haven't budged. At 3.8% headline and 3.3% core, inflation sits nearly two percentage points above the Fed's 2% target, and the path lower has stalled out for months.
The composition is what should worry investors most. Strip out food, energy, and housing, and core services rose just 0.2%, suggesting underlying demand-driven inflation is contained. The pressure is coming from energy spillovers tied to the Iran war and tariff effects feeding through to goods prices. Housing, which the Fed has long expected to cool, is heating up instead. That mix is harder for monetary policy to fix.
Income data adds another wrinkle. Wages held up, but the saving rate at 2.6% is historically low, and real disposable income is shrinking. Consumers kept spending in April, up 0.5%, but they're funding it by saving less while their purchasing power erodes. That's not a setup that lasts.
Markets have already moved on from rate cuts this year. Traders are pricing the Fed's next move as a potential hike in early 2027, not a cut. For positioning, that means longer-duration Treasuries stay vulnerable, financials benefit from a higher-for-longer curve, and consumer discretionary names face a squeeze if real income keeps slipping. The first-quarter GDP revision down to 1.6% from 2% reinforces the stagflation-lite backdrop: growth softer than expected, inflation stickier than hoped.
