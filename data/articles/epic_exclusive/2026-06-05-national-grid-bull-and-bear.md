National Grid Bull and Bear

The Motley Fool Team

Jun 5, 2026, 4:44 PM

Comments

National Grid plc (NYSE:NGG) is one of the world’s largest investor-owned utilities, operating electricity and gas networks across the United Kingdom and northeastern United States. The company’s strategy is focused on regulated grid infrastructure, with an unprecedented £70 billion five-year investment plan targeting electrical grid modernization, integration of renewable energy, and enabling increased demand from data centers, electrified transport, and heating. With a progressive dividend policy and a renewed focus on stable, inflation-linked earnings, National Grid aims to lead the energy transition while delivering resilient shareholder returns.

Potential Bull Cases
1. Unmatched Capital Investment Fuels Accelerated Asset and Earnings Growth

National Grid’s five-year £70 billion investment program is driving double-digit regulated asset base growth and robust earnings expansion. The company has entered the largest capex cycle in its history, with 2026 capital investment reaching a record £11.6 billion—up 18% year over year, and guidance of £13 billion for 2027. This capital is earmarked for high-return, regulated projects, with 85% meeting EU Taxonomy green criteria. Asset growth averaged nearly 11% in 2026 and is projected to compound at around 10% per year through 2031, leading to underlying earnings per share (EPS) growth of 8–10% annually. This outsized investment is already being borne out in financial results: underlying operating profit rose 9% at constant currency to £5.68 billion in FY2026, while return on equity improved to 9.8%. The high degree of regulatory visibility—two-thirds of planned capex is already approved—mitigates execution risk and underpins investor confidence in this runway for earnings and asset expansion.

2. Regulatory Frameworks Secure Long-Term, Inflation-Linked Returns

National Grid benefits from stable, transparent regulatory regimes in both the UK and US, anchoring earnings and providing strong protection against macroeconomic shocks. With the acceptance of the RIIO-T3 price control in the UK (effective until 2031) and major rate settlements secured in New York and Massachusetts through 2028, the company has locked in the bulk of allowed returns for the next five years. These frameworks offer inflation indexation for both revenues and regulated asset base, effectively creating a natural hedge against rising costs and supporting real, inflation-protected equity returns. About 80% of the company’s debt is fixed rate, and regulatory trackers in the US allow for timely cost recovery. This level of earnings and capital visibility is unusual for global utilities and reduces risks from market volatility, wage and material inflation, and interest rate swings—affording long-term investors a resilient income stream.

3. Electrification and AI-Driven Demand Spur New Growth Opportunities

Secular shifts in electrification, particularly from AI data centers and decarbonization, are powering sustained underlying demand growth for National Grid’s networks. By March 2031, the company expects to connect up to 19 gigawatts of new demand—about half linked to data centers—in the UK. UK data center energy consumption is forecast to rise from 2.6% of total demand today to 9% by 2035, supporting medium-term grid expansion. National Grid’s unique transatlantic footprint positions it to capitalize on these growth trends, with more than 75% of its five-year plan supported by regulatory delivery mechanisms or contracts already in place. Ongoing collaboration with government and technology firms ensures that new sources of electrical demand, such as AI and high-capacity computing, translate directly into predictable, regulated returns.

4. Dividend Growth Remains Progressive and Well-Covered

A robust dividend policy, with a coverage of 1.6x, continues to distinguish National Grid as an income stalwart. The company declared a full-year dividend of 48.49p per share for 2026, up 3.8% year over year and precisely tracking UK CPIH inflation as per policy. National Grid has historically prioritized dividend growth, with distributable reserves of £17.0 billion—sufficient to cover more than five years of forecast payouts. Notably, the board reaffirmed underlying earnings per share CAGR guidance of 8–10% to 2031 while maintaining strict discipline on credit metrics (FFO/net debt at 13.0%).

5. Digitalization and Innovation Enhance Operational Resilience and Value Creation

Investments in AI and digital grid technologies are advancing operational efficiency and future-proofing National Grid’s business model. Initiatives like the partnership with Air Space Intelligence to deploy operational AI are designed to optimize grid asset management, anticipate outages, and improve demand forecasting—addressing new complexities from distributed generation, electric vehicles, and variable renewables. The company is also scaling up autonomous drone inspections, digital job briefs to enhance safety, and smart meter rollouts (over 1.3 million installed in 2025/26). These advances not only improve reliability (over 99.9% network availability) but also support regulatory incentives tied to performance and innovation. By adopting cutting-edge technologies, the company is strengthening its competitive moat as the preferred partner for customers, regulators, and system operators across both the UK and US.

Potential Bear Cases
1. Regulatory Risk Could Weigh on Allowed Returns and Investment Pace

Adverse regulatory decisions remain a persistent risk to National Grid’s growth outlook, especially as capex needs accelerate. The regulatory environment, while supportive, is not without challenges. In 2026, the US Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) ordered a reduction in base allowed return on equity (RoE) for New England transmission—triggering customer refunds and putting pressure on profitability in that segment (New England operating profit declined 1% year over year). National Grid is contesting the ruling, but if trends toward lower allowed returns persist in any core jurisdiction, it could slow the pace of future investment, squeeze margins, or require the company to raise external capital on less favorable terms. Similar political and regulatory debates in both the UK and US regarding network affordability and “windfall profits” underscore the vigilance required to maintain constructive relationships with regulators and avoid future adverse interventions.

2. Execution and Supply Chain Risks Threaten Delivery of Record-Scale Capex Program

The sheer scale of National Grid’s £70 billion investment pipeline exposes it to unprecedented execution risks that could delay or inflate costs. Delivering nearly double the historical capex run-rate requires flawless project management, robust supply chains, and sufficient skilled labor across two continents. Risks include construction delays, cost overruns, and third-party renewable project slippages—which already contributed to a year-over-year drop in new UK renewables capacity connected in 2026. While three-quarters of capex is now underpinned by supply agreements or delivery contracts, persistent inflation, geopolitical uncertainty, or shortages in key materials and contractors could erode returns or force the company to reprioritize projects—potentially undermining the growth and decarbonization narrative that underlies the bull case.

3. Political and Policy Uncertainty May Disrupt the Energy Transition and Dampen Investor Returns

Evolving government policies on decarbonization, affordability, and future gas infrastructure create long-term uncertainty that could hinder National Grid’s core growth thesis. National Grid’s forward momentum depends on a stable policy backdrop supporting electrification, grid expansion, and cost recovery—for both new and legacy assets. However, political shifts in the UK or US Northeast (where debates on network charges, net zero mandates, and cost of living are growing louder) may delay project approvals, restrict future allowed returns, or impose new obligations that alter the risk-reward equation. For example, the company’s Scope 1 and 2 emissions rose outside planned targets in 2026 due to weather-driven generation on Long Island, highlighting how climate-related and public policy risks can intersect. Any policy retrenchment or public backlash, particularly around affordability, could lead to tougher investment conditions or more aggressive regulatory resets, placing medium-term returns under pressure.
