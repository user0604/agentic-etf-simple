Bonds Are Back to Singing the Blues

Bonds had performed solidly since a historically horrendous year in 2022. But rising rates have flattened bonds so far in 2026. What should you do with the safer side of your portfolio?

Robert Brokamp, CFP

May 22, 2026, 1:31 PM

Comments (2)
8.1

The last two decades have been a historically interesting time for interest rates and the bond market.

The Great Recession that began in 2007 kicked off a decline in rates that hit rock bottom during the pandemic of 2020. Those low rates fanned the flames of inflation that were ignited by government stimulus, broken supply chains, and people emerging from lockdown ready to spend, spend, spend.

In response, the Federal Reserve engaged in the most aggressive rate hiking since the 1980s. As rates rise, bond prices fall -- resulting in a 13% decline for the bond market in 2022, by some measures the worst year for bonds in U.S. history.

Over the past few years, rates went up and down but stayed within a range. However, since the start of the war in Iran on Feb. 28, Treasury rates have been pushing the upper bands of that range. Earlier this week, the yield on the 10-year Treasury reached 4.6% and the yield on the 30-year Treasury over 5.2% -- the highest rate in two decades.

10-Year Treasury Rate data by YCharts.

Perhaps most noteworthy is that the yield on the two‑year Treasury has spiked from 3.4% before the start of the war to over 4%. The two-year Treasury is often considered the bond market's prediction of where the Federal Reserve will or should be headed. If it's right, the Fed is more likely to increase the federal funds rate (the rate the Fed has most direct control over) than to reduce it over the next year or so.

Two-Year Treasury Rate data by YCharts.

This isn't just an American story. Yields in Germany are at their highest since 2011, U.K. rates haven't been this elevated since 2008, and Japan is seeing levels not reached since 1997.

The global bond market is likely responding to rising inflation and rising government indebtedness, both of which can increase the riskiness of bonds. This results in some investors selling their bonds and prospective investors demanding higher rates to compensate for the risks.

This rise in rates has led to muted returns (and even losses) so far in 2026. As you can see from the table below, exchange-traded funds (ETFs) that invest in short-term bonds have held up better than those holding intermediate- to long-term bonds.

What to Do With the Safer Side of Your Portfolio

Back in 2018, Warren Buffett said in an interview with CNBC's Becky Quick, "There's nobody whose predictions on interest rates I would pay attention to, even myself." No one can say for sure where rates will go. But we can still make some investment decisions based on current conditions and educated guesses.

The first key condition is that, unlike most of the time over the past few years, investors do receive extra yield for holding bonds with longer maturities. The trade-off is that a higher yield comes with higher volatility.

And the second key condition is that inflation will likely continue to stay elevated until the Straight of Hormuz is reopened (or markets find a way around it). This will make it more difficult for new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh to cut rates as much as perhaps he and President Donald Trump would like.

Given those conditions and acknowledging that they can always change, here are some considerations for the non-stock portion of your portfolio.

1. Invest in Cash or Cash Equivalents for Money You Need in the Next Year or Two

Don't settle for the piddling rates paid by your local bank. Look for high-yield savings accounts, such as those highlighted by Motley Fool Money.

Money market funds are another option. According to Crane Data, the highest-yielding funds have yields at or close to 3.7%. For those in high-tax states, consider a money market fund that invests only in federal government securities, the income from which is free of state income taxes.

Many investors have been using short-term Treasury bill ETFs as cash equivalents, such as iShares 0-3 Month Treasury Bond (NYSE: SGOV) and Vanguard 0-3 Month Treasury Bill (NASDAQ: VBIL), both of which currently yield 3.6%. Because they invest in Treasuries, the payouts are free of state income taxes. However, there is some slight price volatility, especially right after the ETFs make their monthly distributions.

2. Choose Individual Bonds or CDs for Money You Need in the Next Five Years

The challenge with bond funds is that you don't know what they'll be worth in the future. If rates are higher when you need the money, the fund could actually be worth less than your original investment.

However, when you invest in individual bonds or certificates of deposit (CDs), you'll know exactly how much you'll receive when the bond or CD matures -- regardless of where rates are.

Buying individual bonds is a bit different from buying stocks. To learn more, read "Why and How to Buy Individual Bonds."

3. Get Most of the Best of Both Worlds With Target-Maturity Bond ETFs

Also known as defined-duration or defined-maturity bond ETFs, these funds own only bonds that mature in the same year, which is included in the name of the fund. Over the course of that year, the bonds mature and the fund holds the cash. At the end of the year, after all the bonds have matured, the cash is distributed to the shareholders.

In most cases, when these funds mature, they are close to their original net asset value (NAV) at inception, though it varies by fund. Therefore, they provide more predictability about future value than regular bond funds while also offering diversification, liquidity, and professional management. To learn more, read "7 Boons of Defined-Maturity Bond Funds" and visit the websites of the three biggest issuers: Invesco, iShares, and (very recently) Vanguard.

4. Choose Short- to Intermediate-Term Bond Funds for Portfolio Diversification

If you're investing in bonds to lower the overall risk of your portfolio -- and not necessarily to cover expenses you'll incur in the next few years -- then low-cost bond funds can fit the bill.

However, it's best to stick with short-term bonds (maturities of one to three years) and intermediate-term bonds (four to 10 years). While long-term bonds have somewhat higher yields, they are very sensitive to changes in interest rates and can thus be quite volatile. The risk-reward ratio generally isn't worth it.
